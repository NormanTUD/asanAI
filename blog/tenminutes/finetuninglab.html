The assistant is made after pretraining.

A pretrained model is a very good internet simulator. It does not yet follow instructions, refuse harmful requests, or hold a conversation. Post-training is how it becomes an assistant.

01 From raw text to instructions

Teaching the format.

Pretraining teaches next-token prediction on a huge corpus. The result is fluent but uncontrolled. Supervised fine-tuning then shows the model examples of instructions and good answers, teaching it the format of a conversation.

Fine-tuning is not only about facts. It changes behaviour: when to answer, when to refuse, how to structure a response, and which tone to use.

02 Preferences and reasoning

Aligning behaviour.

Preference optimization goes further. Humans (or a model) compare answers, and the model is trained to prefer the better one. RLHF uses a reward model; DPO-style methods learn directly from preference pairs.

Reasoning models add another stage: reinforcement learning on problems with verifiable answers, so the model learns to think longer and check its work. The total post-training cost is small compared with pretraining, but it shapes most of what users experience.

03 The data and the cost

Preferences are data.

Instruction data teaches the format. A good instruction set is diverse, specific, and representative of the behaviours you want. Quality matters more than raw size: a small set of excellent examples can outperform a large set of noisy ones.

Preference data teaches judgment. Humans or a model compare pairs of answers, and the model learns to prefer the better one. RLHF uses a reward model; DPO-style methods learn directly from the pairs. The choice affects stability, cost, and the kind of behaviour you get.

Reasoning RL adds a third kind of data: problems with verifiable answers. The model learns to think longer and check its work. The post-training cost is small compared with pretraining, but it shapes most of what users experience. Alignment is not solved.

Distillation: What Transfers, and What Doesn't

The analytic extreme skips the student altogether. Rationale distillation extracts a teacher's reasoning; mechanistic interpretability tries to read the algorithm out of the weights directly — induction heads, the modular-addition circuit in a grokked transformer, sparse-autoencoder feature dictionaries. Where output distillation compresses behaviour, circuit-level work compresses mechanism, and its results feed back into evaluation, model editing, and safety. Distillation is also the centre of a persistent intellectual-property fight: most closed-model terms prohibit training competitors on their outputs, and the open web is now saturated with model-generated text, making the provenance of “human” data increasingly hard to establish.

Stage 0: Pretraining (recap)

The base model is trained on the next-token-prediction objective (see the Intuition chapter) over ~10T tokens of web text. The result is a model that completes sentences in the style of its training data, but does not behave as an assistant.

Stage 1: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) teaches it to follow instructions. Stage 2: Preference optimization aligns its outputs with human values. Stage 3: Reasoning RL (optional) teaches it to think longer on hard problems.

The fine print

What post-training cannot fix.

Post-training can shape behaviour, but it cannot add knowledge the base model never saw or guarantee that the model will always follow the rules. A model can learn the format of a refusal and still fail the substance of the request.

Alignment is also a data problem. The preferences you collect, the annotators you hire, and the rubric you use decide what the model learns. Post-training is powerful, but it is not a substitute for a good base model, good data, and a careful evaluation.
