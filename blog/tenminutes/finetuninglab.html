The assistant is made after pretraining.

A pretrained model is a very good internet simulator. It does not yet follow instructions, refuse harmful requests, or hold a conversation. Post-training is how it becomes an assistant.

01 From raw text to instructions

Teaching the format.

Pretraining teaches next-token prediction on a huge corpus. The result is fluent but uncontrolled. Supervised fine-tuning then shows the model examples of instructions and good answers, teaching it the format of a conversation (Ouyang et al., 2022).

Fine-tuning is not only about facts. It changes behaviour: when to answer, when to refuse, how to structure a response, and which tone to use.

02 Preferences and reasoning

Aligning behaviour.

Preference optimization goes further. Humans (or a model) compare answers, and the model is trained to prefer the better one. RLHF uses a reward model (Christiano et al., 2017); DPO-style methods learn directly from preference pairs (Rafailov et al., 2023).

Reasoning models add another stage: reinforcement learning on problems with verifiable answers (Shao et al., 2024), so the model learns to think longer and check its work. The total post-training cost is small compared with pretraining, but it shapes most of what users experience.

03 The data and the cost

Preferences are data.

Instruction data teaches the format. A good instruction set is diverse, specific, and representative of the behaviours you want. Quality matters more than raw size: a small set of excellent examples can outperform a large set of noisy ones.

Preference data teaches judgment. Humans or a model compare pairs of answers, and the model learns to prefer the better one. RLHF uses a reward model; DPO-style methods learn directly from the pairs. The choice affects stability, cost, and the kind of behaviour you get.

Reasoning RL adds a third kind of data: problems with verifiable answers. The model learns to think longer and check its work. The post-training cost is small compared with pretraining, but it shapes most of what users experience. Alignment is not solved.

Background

LoRA and Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning

Full fine-tuning of a 70B model requires ~1 TB of GPU memory (parameters, gradients, and optimizer state). Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) methods train only a tiny fraction of parameters. The best-known is LoRA (Hu et al., 2021): freeze the original weight matrix and learn two small matrices whose product is added to it. If the original matrix is d × d and the adapter rank is r, the adapter has r(d + d) parameters instead of d², which can be thousands of times fewer. QLoRA (Dettmers et al., 2023) combines this with a quantized base model, so a large model can be adapted on a single GPU. Prompt tuning and prefix tuning take the idea further and learn soft prompt vectors rather than weight updates at all.

Background

Safety-Critical Post-Training

A frontier model in 2025 typically has multiple post-training stages with different objectives: helpful SFT, harmless SFT, helpful DPO, harmless DPO, reasoning RL, and final safety refinement. The stages are not redundant. Helpfulness and harmlessness can pull in different directions, and a preference stage can partly undo a safety stage; the order, the data mix, and the final refinement are part of the design. A model that refuses too broadly is useless; one that refuses too narrowly is unsafe. The boundary is a policy choice, and it is learned from examples like everything else.

Background

Distillation: What Transfers, and What Doesn't

The analytic extreme skips the student altogether. Rationale distillation extracts a teacher's reasoning; mechanistic interpretability tries to read the algorithm out of the weights directly — induction heads, the modular-addition circuit in a grokked transformer, sparse-autoencoder feature dictionaries. Where output distillation compresses behaviour, circuit-level work compresses mechanism, and its results feed back into evaluation, model editing, and safety. Distillation is also the centre of a persistent intellectual-property fight: most closed-model terms prohibit training competitors on their outputs, and the open web is now saturated with model-generated text, making the provenance of “human” data increasingly hard to establish.

More background

Stage 0: Pretraining (recap)

The base model is trained on the next-token-prediction objective (see the Intuition chapter) over ~10T tokens of web text. The result is a model that completes sentences in the style of its training data, but does not behave as an assistant.

Stage 1: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) teaches it to follow instructions. Stage 2: Preference optimization aligns its outputs with human values. Stage 3: Reasoning RL (optional) teaches it to think longer on hard problems.

More background

Stage 2: Reward Modeling (for RLHF)

RLHF first trains a reward model on human comparisons: it learns to give an answer a score. Then a reinforcement-learning algorithm, usually PPO, updates the language model to maximise that score, with a KL penalty that keeps it close to the supervised model so it does not drift into nonsense. DPO skips the separate reward model and learns directly from the preference pairs, which is simpler and often more stable.

Reward hacking (see the Reinforcement Learning chapter) is the constant danger: a sufficiently good optimizer will find ways to maximise reward without improving quality. It may learn to be verbose, to flatter, or to imitate the annotator's style rather than to be correct. Defenses include a KL penalty, an ensemble of reward models, process supervision (rewarding the steps rather than only the final answer), and keeping humans in the loop.

The fine print

What post-training cannot fix.

Post-training can shape behaviour, but it cannot add knowledge the base model never saw or guarantee that the model will always follow the rules. A model can learn the format of a refusal and still fail the substance of the request. It can also forget: narrow instruction data can degrade the general language ability the base model had, a phenomenon called catastrophic forgetting. Labs often mix some pretraining data back into post-training to keep the base capabilities alive.

Alignment is also a data problem. The preferences you collect, the annotators you hire, and the rubric you use decide what the model learns. Post-training is powerful, but it is not a substitute for a good base model, good data, and a careful evaluation.
