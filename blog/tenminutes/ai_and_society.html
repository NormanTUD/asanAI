The social effects are already here.

AI is not only a technical project. It changes how images are made, how work is organised, how news is trusted, and how people imagine the future.

01 A short history of expectations

Hope, winter, spring.

The technical stack built the machine. This page asks what it does to us. The field began with enormous optimism at the Dartmouth workshop in 1956, announced in the 1955 proposal (McCarthy et al., 1955), followed by winters when the promises outran the results. The public relationship has oscillated ever since: hope, disappointment, and another spring.

What is different now is scale. Synthetic images, voices, and text are cheap and abundant. That changes the economics of creation and the cost of deception.

02 Creation, work, and truth

The effects are uneven.

Generative models expand what can be made, but they also flood the world with synthetic media. Voice cloning and deepfakes make fraud and political deception easier (Chesney & Citron, 2019). The same tools can help artists, scientists, and students. The verification problem from hallucination returns here at scale: making a convincing fake is now cheap, while verifying that something is real is still expensive.

There is a second-order effect that is easy to miss: the liar's dividend (Chesney & Citron, 2019). Once people know that fakes exist, genuine evidence can be dismissed as fabricated. A politician caught on tape can claim the tape is AI-generated; a real photograph can be waved away as a deepfake. The cost of deception rises, and so does the cost of believing anything.

The effects on work are uneven. Some tasks are automated; others are augmented; new tasks appear. A job is a bundle of tasks, and a model rarely replaces the whole bundle at once. The distribution of gains and losses matters as much as the average: the same tool can make one role more productive and another redundant, and bargaining power decides who captures the gain.

Bias belongs here too. A model trained on historical data can reproduce historical patterns in hiring, lending, policing, and healthcare, and it can do so at scale while sounding neutral. The technical question is whether the disparity comes from the data, the label, the objective, or the deployment; the political question is which disparities a society is willing to accept.

03 The governance question

Who is accountable?

Synthetic media changes the cost of creation and the cost of deception. Voice cloning and deepfakes make fraud and political manipulation easier. The same tools can help artists, scientists, and students. The effects are uneven and often arrive before the rules.

Work is not a single story. Some tasks are automated, others are augmented, and new tasks appear. The distribution of gains and losses matters as much as the average. Institutions, skills, and bargaining power shape who benefits.

The governance question is about accountability. Who is responsible when a model causes harm? What should be disclosed? How do we verify synthetic content? Regulation, literacy, and transparency are all part of the answer, but each has limits. Provenance standards such as C2PA attach a signed history to a file, yet they only help if the file carries the signature and the viewer checks it. Watermarks can be removed or laundered. Detection is an arms race, and a detector that is right most of the time is not a foundation for evidence. The technology is new; the social questions are old.

Background

The Lie: Deception at Scale

The most immediate and visceral cultural impact of generative AI has been its capacity to lie convincingly. Not to lie in the way a person lies — with intent, with a face, with a voice you can look at — but to lie in the way a photocopy lies: a perfect surface reproduction of the truth that contains no truth. A cloned voice can authorize a transfer; a synthetic photo can place someone at a protest they never attended; a fabricated quote can travel further than its correction. The damage is not only that people believe false things. It is that the shared base of evidence a society argues from becomes harder to trust.

Background

Automation Bias: Trusting the Machine

Automation bias is the tendency to accept an automated suggestion more readily than a human one, and to notice fewer of its errors when the automation is present (Parasuraman & Riley, 1997). It has been documented in aviation, medicine, and forecasting; the human becomes a monitor who monitors less. With LLMs, the “aid” is always confident, always fluent, and always available, which makes the bias stronger. The automation bias is not a bug to be patched; it is a feature of the human cognitive architecture meeting a new kind of tool.

The Question

This lesson traces the cultural impact of that question as it has unfolded over seven decades. Not the technical history — other lessons in this course cover the mathematics and the architecture. This is the story of what happens when a technology becomes cultural: when it stops being a research program and starts being a mirror, a weapon, a tool, a threat, a companion, and a question about what it means to be human.

The story has no single arc. It is not "progress" or "decline." It is a braided, contradictory, still-unfolding story in which the same technology that predicts the structure of every protein in the human body also generates a photorealistic video of a sitting president saying things they never said. The same tool that lets a blind person read a menu independently also lets a criminal clone a CEO's voice and drain a bank account. The same model that discovers a new class of stable materials also learns to write a convincing fake news article.

More background

The Flood: AI Slop and the Verification Crisis

By 2024–2025, the volume of AI-generated content on the internet had reached a scale that broke existing verification infrastructure. The term "AI slop" — low-value, AI-generated content (text, image, video) flooding social feeds — entered common usage.

This is not a problem that will be solved by "better detection." It is a structural change in the information environment, comparable to the transition from oral to written culture (the loss of the "witness" as the unit of truth) or from print to broadcast (the loss of the "author" as the unit of authority). We are in the transition from authored to synthesized culture, and the norms, institutions, and cognitive habits that governed the previous regime are not yet adapted.
