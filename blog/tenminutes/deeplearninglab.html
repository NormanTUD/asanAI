Depth is composition.

One layer can draw a line. Stack layers and each one can build on the features of the layer below: edges become parts, parts become objects, objects become concepts.

01 The idea

A stack of functions.

Overfitting showed why a model can fit the noise. Depth is one way to make flexibility useful rather than dangerous. A deep network is not a wider network. It is a stack of functions: the output of one layer becomes the input of the next. Each layer can reuse and recombine the features it receives.

That is why depth is powerful. A feature that took many simple operations to build can be reused by later layers instead of being relearned from scratch. The network builds a hierarchy, not a pile of independent detectors.

02 Why nonlinearity is essential

The bend prevents the collapse.

If every layer is affine, the whole stack collapses into one affine map. The nonlinearity between layers is what prevents the collapse and allows the composition to create new, curved features.

Deep learning is not magic. It is function composition plus a lot of data, a good loss, and an optimizer that can find useful parameters. Training is the hard part.

Why start with random weights? Because perfect symmetry is a trap. If every weight in a layer began at the same value, every unit would receive the same gradient and stay identical, so the layer would learn one feature many times over. Small random values break the symmetry. The scale matters too: too large and activations explode, too small and the signal vanishes before it reaches the next layer. Initialization is the first place where the arithmetic of depth becomes visible.

03 Why depth is efficient

Reuse and composition.

The universal approximation theorem (Cybenko, 1989; Hornik et al., 1989) says a wide enough network can approximate a broad class of functions. It is an existence result, not a training guarantee: it does not say that gradient descent will find those weights, that the network will generalise, or that the network will be small. Depth offers a different kind of efficiency: a deep network can reuse a feature instead of relearning it at every level, and for some functions depth is provably exponentially more compact than width (Telgarsky, 2016). That is a statement about representation, not a promise that deeper is always better on your data.

A hierarchy is a compression of computation. Edges become parts, parts become objects, objects become concepts. Each level can build on the level below, so the number of parameters needed to represent a complex function can be much smaller than a single wide layer would require.

Depth is not free. Gradients can vanish or explode, activations can become unstable, and training deep networks requires careful initialisation, normalisation, and residual connections. The architecture is powerful, but the optimisation is part of the story.

Background

Why Depth Beats Width: Reuse

The mechanical reason depth is so effective is reuse. A sub-result computed by an early layer is reused by everything downstream. Depth turns "compute, throw away, recompute" into "compute once, reuse everywhere."

This matches how the real world is structured: pixels → edges → parts → objects; sounds → phonemes → words → meaning. Deep networks are compact for the same reason: the world itself is compositional.

Background

How the Knowledge Gets Into the Weights

The clever part is that backprop reuses intermediate results, walking the error signal backward through the network. What would naively require recomputing every path separately becomes a single backward pass whose cost is roughly the same order as the forward pass. That is the algorithmic reason deep networks are trainable at all.

Repeat millions of times. The trajectory of the weights through weight-space is literally what writes the knowledge into the matrices. There is no separate memory — the knowledge is the final numbers. This is also why “the model knows X” is a compressed way of speaking: nothing is stored as a sentence or a fact; the behaviour is a property of the whole matrix.

More background

Why Deep Learning Works (And Why It Works So Well)

Here's the mechanical answer to why "a big pile of matrix multiplications" ends up being able to translate languages, recognize faces, and play chess better than any human. It comes down to four ingredients working together: depth, nonlinearity, backpropagation, and the optimizer's hidden preferences.

Depth supplies composition: later layers reuse the features earlier layers built, so the network can represent a hierarchy without relearning each level. Nonlinearity is what keeps the composition from collapsing into one affine map. Backpropagation assigns credit efficiently: it computes how each of the millions of parameters contributed to the error, at roughly the cost of one extra forward pass.

The optimizer's preferences are subtler: stochastic gradient descent does not merely find any fit; it tends to prefer solutions that are flat and simple in a way that correlates with generalisation. None of the four is sufficient alone. Depth without nonlinearity is linear; nonlinearity without credit assignment cannot learn; credit assignment without an optimizer that generalises can memorize.

More background

Residual Connections: How We Actually Train Really Deep Networks

There's a practical problem with stacking many layers. When backprop sends the error signal backward through, say, 100 layers, it gets multiplied by many small numbers along the way. The signal often shrinks to nearly zero (the "vanishing gradient" problem) or occasionally explodes. Early layers stop learning because they receive no meaningful feedback.

The residual connection is the fix. Instead of forcing the signal through the whole transformation, the layer computes an update and adds it to its input: x → x + f(x). The identity path gives the gradient a direct route back, so even if f is badly scaled, the signal can still reach the early layers. Normalisation keeps the activations in a healthy range, and careful initialisation keeps the variance from compounding. This is why “depth” in practice means residual connections and normalisation, not just more matrix multiplications.

The fine print

What depth costs.

Deep networks are harder to train. Gradients can vanish or explode, activations can drift, and the loss surface can be unfriendly. Residual connections, normalisation, careful initialisation, and learning-rate schedules are not decoration; they are what make depth practical.

Depth also costs compute and data. A deeper model is not automatically better; it needs enough signal to learn the hierarchy. The architecture is powerful, but the optimisation and the data are part of the result.

The residual stream

Depth is a shared scratchpad.

A residual connection (He et al., 2015) is simple: a layer computes an update and adds it to its input, x → x + f(x). The signal does not have to pass through every transformation; it has a direct path. That makes deep networks easier to train because gradients can flow through the addition.

In a transformer, the same idea becomes the residual stream: one vector per token that every attention head and every MLP reads and writes. Nothing is overwritten. Each block adds its contribution, and the final layer reads the sum.

The optimiser

Walk downhill, one gradient at a time.

Depth gives the network a rich surface of possible behaviours. Training is the walk across the surface of the loss. The gradient points uphill; the optimiser steps the other way. The learning rate is the length of the step, and it is the difference between slow progress, a clean descent, and a jump that overshoots the valley.
