A tiny example network.

Before attention and transformers, there is a line. A single neuron learns by adjusting two numbers until its guess is less wrong.

01 The idea

A line with two knobs.

Take one input x, multiply it by a weight a, add a bias b, and you have a prediction y = ax + b. The weight decides the slope; the bias shifts the line. That is the entire model.

Learning is the search for better a and b. Show the model an example, compare its prediction with the target, and measure the error. The error is a number; the learning algorithm uses it to nudge the two knobs.

02 From one line to a network

The loop repeats.

A single affine map is not yet a full neural network. Stack many of these maps, put a nonlinearity between them, and you get a network that can bend. Without the nonlinearity, the stack collapses back into one line.

The smallest learner is still the right place to start. Every larger model repeats the same loop at a different scale: predict, measure the error, adjust the parameters.

03 The learning loop

Gradient descent in miniature.

The error is a number. For a line, the mean squared error is the average of the squared vertical distances between the points and the line. That number defines a surface: every choice of a and b gives a point on the surface, and the height is the error.

The gradient is the slope of that surface. It tells you which way to move a and b to make the error smaller. The learning rate decides how far you move. Too small and learning is slow; too large and you overshoot the minimum.

Real training uses batches, epochs, regularisation, and many more parameters, but the loop is the same: predict, measure the error, compute the gradient, nudge the parameters. The toy is small enough to see the loop, not a miniature of a frontier model.

Background

The Statistical Nature of Learning

We often think of Neural Networks as “learning” in the way a human student learns: by understanding concepts. However, mathematically, a Neural Network is simply a statistical machine trying to fit a curve to a distribution.

Background

Data is not just Numbers; it is a Distribution

If our training data is a “representative sample” (meaning it follows the same statistical distribution as the real world example you're trying to model), the weights of the network will converge to the “True” relationship. If the data is biased (a bad sample), the model learns a skewed reality. This is why knowing your data's distribution is critical. You cannot fit a straight line to a circle; you must choose a model architecture that matches the geometry of your data's distribution.

More background

Output Layer: The Mirror of the Target

The final layer of a neural network is not arbitrary; it is a mathematical mirror of the data you want to predict. Its shape, the number of neurons it contains, must match the dimensionality of your “Labels” (the ground truth).

More background

Spatial and Complex Outputs

If the output layer's dimensions do not match the target data's dimensions, the Loss Function will be unable to compare the prediction to the reality, and the “loop” of learning will break.

Practical notes

From two knobs to billions.

The toy has one input and one output. A real network has many inputs, many outputs, and many layers. The output layer has to match the target: a number, a class, a probability distribution, or a spatial map. If the output does not match the data, the loss cannot compare the prediction with reality.

Training uses batches, epochs, validation, regularisation, and hardware. The loop is the same, but the engineering is not. The toy is a lens on the loop, not a miniature of a frontier model.
