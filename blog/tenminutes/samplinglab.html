The distribution is not the answer.

A language model ends with a probability for every token. Decoding is the rule that picks one. Change the rule and the same model sounds focused, repetitive, or wild.

01 Temperature and greedy

Sharp or flat.

The transformer ended with a distribution. Now choose from it. The model ends with a vector of logits, one score per vocabulary token. Softmax turns those scores into probabilities (the same softmax as at the end of a classifier). Temperature divides the logits before the softmax: below one the gaps between scores widen and the distribution sharpens; above one the gaps shrink and the distribution flattens.

At temperature near zero, the most likely token gets almost all of the mass, so decoding becomes greedy and deterministic. At temperature one, you sample from the model's native distribution. Above one, rare tokens become reachable and the output becomes more surprising, and more likely to be incoherent.

Greedy decoding is not 'the truth'; it is just the highest-probability continuation. It can be repetitive and can miss a better sentence that starts with a less likely token. Temperature changes the shape of the choice, not the model's knowledge.

02 Probability is not truth

The decoder cannot add knowledge.

A high-probability token is not guaranteed to be correct. The model is a likelihood machine: it has learned which continuations are common in its training text, not which ones are true. A confident distribution can still put the wrong token on top.

Decoding changes how the model sounds, not what it knows. Greedy makes it focused and repetitive; sampling makes it varied; filters remove the tail. None of these choices adds a fact-checker or a memory the model did not have.

That is why a decoder setting is a policy decision. For a factual answer you want a tight distribution and a low temperature. For a poem you may want more freedom. The same model can behave like a careful clerk or a wild storyteller because the decoder is a separate layer of control.

03 The candidate filters

Cut the tail before you draw.

Top-k (Fan et al., 2018) keeps the k most likely tokens and renormalises their probabilities. It is simple and fast: k = 1 is greedy, k = 50 is a common chat setting, k = 1000 is closer to open-ended sampling. The weakness is that k is fixed. Sometimes only three tokens are plausible; sometimes a hundred are. A fixed k cannot adapt to how sharp or flat the distribution is.

Top-p, or nucleus sampling (Holtzman et al., 2020), keeps the smallest set of tokens whose cumulative probability reaches p. If one token already dominates, the set is tiny; if the distribution is flat, the set is large. That is the point: the candidate pool expands when the model is uncertain and contracts when it is confident. Typical values are p = 0.9 or 0.95; p = 0.5 is very focused.

Min-p (Nguyen et al., 2025) is newer and scales with confidence. It keeps every token whose probability is at least s times the probability of the top token. With s = 0.1, any token within a factor of ten of the leader survives; with s = 1.0 only the leader survives. The method is available in several inference stacks and often behaves well at higher temperatures, where top-p alone can keep too much of the tail.

The readout in the demo also shows entropy, a single number for how spread out the distribution is. Low entropy means the model is confident and the choice is nearly forced; high entropy means many continuations are plausible. Entropy is useful because it tells you whether the distribution itself is sharp, independently of where you set the filter.

04 Practice

Greedy is not the truth.

Greedy decoding is deterministic, fast, and often a good default for code or factual retrieval, but it can loop, because the most likely continuation is always the same continuation. Pure sampling is the opposite failure: the long tail stays reachable, so the text can wander. The filters keep the tail out while preserving choice. Temperature and the filter are not independent: a high temperature flattens the distribution, then the filter decides how much of that flatness survives.

There is no universal setting. Code and facts often want a low temperature and a tight filter; brainstorming wants more diversity; dialogue sits in between. Change one knob, test it, and keep what works for the task.

One alternative deserves a mention: beam search (Sutskever et al., 2014) keeps several partial sequences alive at once and chooses the complete sequence with the best total score. It is useful when there is a single good answer and the score is reliable, as in some translation or speech tasks. It is a poor fit for open-ended conversation, where the highest-probability sequence is often bland, repetitive, or simply not what a person would say.

The practical rule

The model supplies the distribution; the decoder supplies the policy.

Temperature, top-k, top-p, and min-p are different answers to the same question: how much surprise should the next token be allowed to carry?

05 Beyond the filter

Repetition, search, speculation.

Temperature and truncation are the first knobs, not the only ones. A repetition penalty lowers the score of tokens that already appeared; DRY (Don't Repeat Yourself) penalises repeated sequences rather than single tokens; XTC removes the most obvious choices to increase diversity. Contrastive search balances likelihood against novelty, and mirostat targets a fixed perplexity instead of a fixed candidate set. Each is a different answer to the same problem: the model's most likely continuation is often bland or repetitive.

Search changes the shape of the whole sequence. Beam search keeps several partial continuations alive and picks the best complete one; it is useful when there is one good answer and the score is reliable, as in some translation tasks, and a poor fit for open-ended conversation. Speculative sampling uses a small draft model to propose several tokens, then verifies them with the large model; it speeds up generation without changing the target distribution.

Practical starting points: code and factual answers at temperature 0 to 0.3 with top-p 0.9; chat at 0.7 to 1.0 with top-p 0.9; creative writing at 1.0 or higher with top-p 0.95 and a small min-p. These are starting points, not laws. Test on your own prompts and keep the setting that works.
