Sound becomes tokens.

A waveform is too dense to feed directly into a language model. Audio systems compress it into representations that a transformer can treat like a sequence.

01 Three ways to represent sound

From waveform to codec.

Multimodal models joined pixels and words. The same move works for sound. The raw waveform is a long list of numbers: tens of thousands of samples per second. A spectrogram turns it into a picture of frequency over time. A neural audio codec compresses it into discrete tokens; self-supervised models learn representations without labels. The sample rate is already a design choice: speech is usually captured at 16 kHz because most of the information needed to understand words sits below 8 kHz, while music is captured at 44.1 kHz or 48 kHz because it uses the higher frequencies.

Modern systems often use codec tokens because they behave like a vocabulary. Once sound is a sequence of tokens, the same transformer machinery that handles text can listen, transcribe, and generate. A codec does not simply round the waveform; it learns a small set of reusable acoustic units, often with several “codebooks” stacked, and sends the indices of those units. That is what makes the output discrete enough to predict.

02 Listening and speaking

The same machinery, a different front end.

Speech recognition maps audio tokens to text tokens. Text-to-speech goes the other way. Music generation predicts audio tokens with a model that has learned long-range structure.

The hard part is not only the architecture. Audio is noisy, speakers vary, and timing matters. Generated speech can sound completely natural and still mispronounce the one word that matters, because the model predicts acoustics rather than reading a dictionary. Evaluation has to match the task: word error rate for transcription, intelligibility and naturalness for speech, musical structure for generation.
