Sound becomes tokens.

A waveform is too dense to feed directly into a language model. Audio systems compress it into representations that a transformer can treat like a sequence.

01 Three ways to represent sound

From waveform to codec.

Multimodal models joined pixels and words. This page does the same for sound. The raw waveform is a long list of numbers: tens of thousands of samples per second. A spectrogram turns it into a picture of frequency over time. A neural audio codec compresses it into discrete tokens; self-supervised models learn representations without labels. The sample rate is already a design choice: speech is usually captured at 16 kHz because most of the information needed to understand words sits below 8 kHz, while music is captured at 44.1 kHz or 48 kHz because it uses the higher frequencies.

Modern systems often use codec tokens because they behave like a vocabulary. Once sound is a sequence of tokens, the same transformer machinery that handles text can listen, transcribe, and generate. A codec does not simply round the waveform; it learns a small set of reusable acoustic units, often with several “codebooks” stacked, and sends the indices of those units. That is what makes the output discrete enough to predict.

02 Listening and speaking

The same machinery, a different front end.

Speech recognition maps audio tokens to text tokens. Text-to-speech goes the other way. Music generation predicts audio tokens with a model that has learned long-range structure.

The hard part is not only the architecture. Audio is noisy, speakers vary, and timing matters. The model needs a representation that preserves the information people actually use.

03 Why audio is hard

Noise, timing, and meaning.

Audio is noisy. A waveform contains the speaker, the room, the microphone, and the background at the same time. The model has to separate what matters from what does not. Speakers vary in pitch, accent, and speed, and the same word can sound different every time.

Timing matters. Speech is not a bag of sounds; the order and duration carry meaning. Music adds long-range structure: rhythm, harmony, and repetition. A model that predicts the next audio token has to learn structure at several timescales.

Speech recognition, text-to-speech, and music generation have different objectives and different evaluations. Intelligibility, naturalness, and meaning are not the same thing. The representation has to preserve the information people actually use.

Background

Speech Recognition: Whisper and Friends

The model is trained with a multi-task objective: transcribe, translate to English, detect language, timestamp. Whisper was trained on 680,000 hours of weakly supervised audio; the 2023 large-v3 checkpoint of the model described by Radford et al. (2022) achieves under 3% word error rate on clean English and ~10% on noisy multilingual. Word error rate is the edit distance between the transcript and the reference, divided by the number of reference words: substitutions, insertions, and deletions all count. A 3% WER still means roughly one wrong word in thirty-three, which matters for names, numbers, and anything legally binding.

Conformer (Gulati et al., 2020) is the dominant architecture for streaming ASR. It combines convolutions (for local patterns) with self-attention (for global context) in each block. USM (Google, 2023) and SeamlessM4T (Meta, 2023) extend this to 100+ languages.

From Waveform to Tokens: Three Representations

A raw audio waveform is a 1-D signal sampled at 16 kHz or 44.1 kHz, a sequence of 16,000 to 44,100 floats per second. That is too dense for any model to ingest directly. Modern systems compress audio through one of three representations:

More background

1. Spectrograms (and Mel-spectrograms)

A spectrogram is a 2-D representation: time on the x-axis, frequency on the y-axis, intensity as colour. It is computed by Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT): split the waveform into 20–40 ms windows, compute the magnitude of the FFT, stack them.

More background

2. Neural Audio Codecs

A neural codec learns an encoder that turns a short window of audio into a latent vector, a quantizer that snaps that vector to the nearest entry in a learned codebook, and a decoder that reconstructs the waveform. The token is the codebook index. Because the decoder is trained to preserve what listeners hear, the tokens keep the information that matters for speech and music while discarding the raw sample-by-sample detail. This is the representation that makes audio look like text to a transformer.

More background

3. Self-Supervised Representations

Models like wav2vec 2.0, HuBERT, and WavLM learn speech representations by masking spans of audio and predicting the discrete latent units of a teacher model. The output is a sequence of 50 Hz contextualized vectors, perfect input for downstream ASR, speaker ID, or emotion recognition. The difference from a codec is the objective: a codec is trained to reconstruct the sound, a self-supervised model is trained to predict what was masked.

Practical notes

Audio is not text.

Speech models can fail on accents, background noise, overlapping speakers, and rare names. Music models can produce pleasant textures that lack long-range structure. Generated speech can sound natural and still mispronounce the important word. That last failure is structural: a text-to-speech model predicts acoustics, not a dictionary pronunciation, so a rare name or a heteronym can be voiced with complete confidence and the wrong phonemes. Systems often add a pronunciation lexicon or a separate grapheme-to-phoneme step for exactly this reason.

Evaluation has to match the task: word error rate for transcription, intelligibility and naturalness for speech, musical structure for generation. A single score is not enough.
