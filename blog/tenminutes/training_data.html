A model is what it reads.

Architecture gets the headlines; data decides much of the behaviour. The mixture, quality, and cleanliness of the training corpus are half the secret.

01 The scale and the mix

Trillions of tokens.

Learning was about the learner. This page is about its diet. Frontier models are trained on trillions of tokens. The bulk comes from the web, with code, books, scientific papers, and reference text mixed in. Each source brings a different style and a different kind of knowledge. To make the number concrete: a token is roughly three quarters of an English word, so ten trillion tokens is on the order of seven trillion words — more than any person could read in a thousand lifetimes, and on the order of large estimates of all the books ever published.

The mixture is a design choice. Too much web text and the model learns noise and spam; too little code and it cannot program; too little long-form text and it struggles with structure. The word “choice” is literal: labs tune the proportions, and changing them changes what the model is good at.

02 Cleaning is part of the model

Quality, dedupe, decontamination.

Raw web text is messy. Pipelines filter for quality, remove duplicates, and try to remove test sets that would contaminate later benchmarks. Deduplication matters because repeated text can make a model memorize instead of generalize: if the same paragraph appears a thousand times, the cheapest way to reduce loss is to store it, not to learn the pattern behind it. Duplicates also waste compute and make the corpus look larger than its effective diversity.

Contamination is the benchmark version of the same problem. If examples from a test set appear in training, a high score may measure recall rather than ability. The leak can be direct (the exact question) or indirect (a paraphrase, a solution posted online, or the same underlying document). Detecting it is hard, which is why benchmark claims deserve a second look.

Data recipes are closely guarded. The same architecture trained on a better corpus can outperform a larger model trained on a worse one. Synthetic data can fill gaps, but it can also amplify the generator's biases and, at the limit, cause model collapse (Shumailov et al., 2023). Much web text is also copyrighted, some of it personal; whether training on it is fair use is a live dispute, not a settled technical detail.

How is “quality” scored? Usually by a stack of imperfect proxies: length, punctuation, and repetition heuristics; a classifier trained on pages humans rated as useful; perplexity under a reference model; and spot checks. None is neutral — a filter trained on one kind of writing quietly prefers that kind, which is one way a corpus acquires a house style. When a model behaves strangely, the training data is often part of the explanation.
