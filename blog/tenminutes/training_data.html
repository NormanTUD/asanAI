A model is what it reads.

Architecture gets the headlines; data decides much of the behaviour. The mixture, quality, and cleanliness of the training corpus are half the secret.

01 The scale and the mix

Trillions of tokens.

Frontier models are trained on trillions of tokens. The bulk comes from the web, with code, books, scientific papers, and reference text mixed in. Each source brings a different style and a different kind of knowledge. To make the number concrete: a token is roughly three quarters of an English word, so ten trillion tokens is on the order of seven trillion words — more than any person could read in a thousand lifetimes, and on the order of large estimates of all the books ever published.

The mixture is a design choice. Too much web text and the model learns noise and spam; too little code and it cannot program; too little long-form text and it struggles with structure. The word “choice” is literal: labs tune the proportions, and changing them changes what the model is good at.

02 Cleaning is part of the model

Quality, dedupe, decontamination.

Raw web text is messy. Pipelines filter for quality, remove duplicates, and try to remove test sets that would contaminate later benchmarks. Deduplication matters because repeated text can make a model memorize instead of generalize: if the same paragraph appears a thousand times, the cheapest way to reduce loss is to store it, not to learn the pattern behind it. Duplicates also waste compute and make the corpus look larger than its effective diversity.

Contamination is the benchmark version of the same problem. If examples from a test set appear in training, a high score may measure recall rather than ability. The leak can be direct (the exact question) or indirect (a paraphrase, a solution posted online, or the same underlying document). Detecting it is hard, which is why benchmark claims deserve a second look.

Data recipes are closely guarded. The same architecture trained on a better corpus can outperform a larger model trained on a worse one. When a model behaves strangely, the training data is often part of the explanation.

03 The mixture is a design choice

Quality and quantity both matter.

The source mix is a design choice. Web text gives scale and variety; code gives structure and procedural reasoning; books give long-form argument; papers give precise claims; reference text gives compact facts; synthetic math gives targeted practice. Changing the mix changes the model's behaviour.

Quality and quantity are not opposites. A small, clean corpus is not enough to train a frontier model; a huge, noisy corpus is not enough either. Curation can make a smaller corpus more useful, but it does not replace scale. A frontier model needs both a large and a well-filtered corpus.

Synthetic data can fill gaps, but it can also amplify the biases of the generator. Safety filtering is another layer of design. The useful question is not which one wins, but what mixture and what filtering the model needs for the behaviour you want.

How is “quality” actually scored? Usually by a stack of imperfect proxies: heuristics such as length, punctuation, and repetition; a classifier trained on pages humans rated as useful; perplexity under a reference model; and spot checks by people. None of these is neutral. A filter trained on one kind of writing will quietly prefer that kind, which is one way a corpus acquires a house style.

There is also a legal and ethical layer. Much web text is copyrighted, some of it is personal, and not every page wants to be crawled. Whether training on it is fair use, how consent should work, and what should be excluded are live disputes, not settled technical details. The model's behaviour is shaped by those decisions even though they happen far from the architecture diagram.

The balance

Quality without quantity cannot train a frontier model. Quantity without quality teaches it noise.

More background

Common Crawl: The Foundation

Common Crawl (commoncrawl.org) is a non-profit that has been archiving web pages since 2008. Each month, it crawls ~3 billion URLs, producing ~300 TB of raw HTML. After deduplication and WARC-packaging, this yields ~30 TB of useful text per month.

More background

Quality Filtering

Web text is mostly junk: SEO spam, machine-translated articles, product listings with 50 keyword variations, “Lorem ipsum”, repetitive template content. A model trained on this learns to mimic junk.

More background

Data Mixing: The Frontier Secret

Dolma (AI2, 2024), RedPajama-V2 (Together, 2024), and DataComp-LM (DCLM, 2024) are open efforts to systematically study data mixes. DataComp-LM showed that a 7B model trained on a carefully-filtered 5T tokens can outperform a model trained on 5× more data with weak filtering.
