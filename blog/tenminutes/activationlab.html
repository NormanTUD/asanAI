The bend makes depth meaningful.

A dense layer can only stretch, rotate, and shift. The activation function after it is what lets a network learn curves, folds, and decisions.

01 The gate after the projection

A nonlinearity per coordinate.

A dense layer computes an affine map, Ax + b. On its own it maps straight lines to straight lines. A nonlinear activation is applied to each coordinate after the projection and breaks that linearity.

ReLU passes positive values and sends everything negative to zero. Sigmoid and tanh squash the output into a bounded range. GELU is a smooth variant used in many transformers.

02 Why the choice matters

Gradients and probabilities.

The activation decides how information flows and how gradients behave. ReLU is cheap but can produce dead units; sigmoid and tanh saturate; GELU is smooth and works well in deep transformers.

At the output, softmax turns a vector of scores into a probability distribution. It is not just a normalisation: it is the gate that turns the model's internal scores into a choice over the vocabulary.

03 What the activation changes

Gradients and geometry.

A saturating activation such as sigmoid or tanh can make gradients very small when the input is far from zero. That slows learning in deep networks. ReLU avoids saturation on the positive side, but it can produce dead units that never activate again.

The shape of the activation changes the geometry of the representation. ReLU creates a hard fold; sigmoid and tanh bend smoothly; GELU is smooth but not monotonic. Modern models also use SiLU/Swish and gated variants such as SwiGLU, which let the network learn how much of a feature to pass through.

At the output, softmax turns scores into a probability distribution. The choice of activation is not a detail: it interacts with normalisation, depth, and the gradients that make training possible.

Background

Why ReLU Works: Sparse Representations

The most common explanation for ReLU's success is that its derivative is 1 for positive inputs and 0 for negative ones, which prevents the vanishing gradient problem. But there is a deeper insight: ReLU creates sparsity.

For any given input, roughly half of ReLU's neurons output exactly zero. This means the network is effectively using a different sparse subnetwork for each input. A neuron that activates for “dog” may be silent for “cat,” and vice versa. Over the entire dataset, each neuron specializes in a subset of patterns, and every input activates only a fraction of the available neurons.

Background

The Breaking Point: Vanishing Gradients

The era of S-shaped curves eventually reached a limit. Because both Sigmoid and Tanh “flatten out” (saturate) at high and low input values, their derivatives become nearly zero. In deep networks, multiplying these tiny numbers together during backpropagation caused the signal to disappear before it reached the earliest layers, a phenomenon known as the vanishing gradient problem.

More background

The Necessity of Activation Functions

Activation functions serve as the mathematical “gates” of a neural network. Without them, a network would be nothing more than a series of linear transformations, essentially collapsing into one giant linear equation.

Why it's not a simple percentage (The “Non-Linear” Secret)

If you input 0.1 and 0.9, you might expect 10% and 90%. Instead, you get roughly 31% and 69%. This is because SoftMax is not a linear scaling; it is an exponential normalization.

By exponentiating the logits, we are measuring the “total growth energy” of all classes combined and then asking: “What percentage of the total energy belongs to Class A?” This ensures that even if a score is negative, it still represents a physical “share” of the total probability, and that the sum of all shares always equals exactly 1.0 (100%).
