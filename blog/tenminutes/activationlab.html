The bend makes depth meaningful.

A dense layer can only stretch, rotate, and shift. The activation function after it is what lets a network learn curves, folds, and decisions.

01 The gate after the projection

A nonlinearity per coordinate.

Depth helps because layers compose. Without a bend, that composition collapses. A dense layer computes an affine map, Ax + b. On its own it maps straight lines to straight lines. A nonlinear activation is applied to each coordinate after the projection and breaks that linearity.

ReLU (Nair & Hinton, 2010) passes positive values and sends everything negative to zero. Sigmoid and tanh squash the output into a bounded range. GELU (Hendrycks & Gimpel, 2016) is a smooth variant used in many transformers.

02 Why the choice matters

Gradients and probabilities.

The activation decides how information flows and how gradients behave. ReLU is cheap but can produce dead units; sigmoid and tanh saturate; GELU is smooth and works well in deep transformers. A dead unit is not a metaphor, if a ReLU neuron's weights drift so that its input is negative for every training example, its gradient is zero forever and it stops learning. Leaky ReLU and GELU avoid the hard zero on the negative side.

At the output, softmax turns a vector of scores into a probability distribution. It is not just a normalisation: it is the gate that turns the model's internal scores into a choice over the vocabulary. Do not confuse it with the hidden-layer activation. ReLU and GELU shape what a unit passes on inside the network; softmax is used at the end because the task is to choose among mutually exclusive tokens.

03 What the bend does to space

Two layers, one map, until the bend.

Write the two dense layers out. The second layer applies its own weights and bias to the output of the first, and the composition can be rearranged into a single affine map.

The product is another weight matrix, and is another bias. So without an activation between them, two dense layers are exactly one dense layer. Depth adds no expressive power; the hidden layer disappears.

The geometric picture comes from Keup and Helias (2022): a layer's job is to fold the data manifold into unused higher dimensions until a flat cut can separate the classes. A ReLU is a hard fold. It is not injective, so it can change the topology of the representation and pull a ring apart. A smooth tanh or sigmoid is a homeomorphism: it bends the space but preserves its topology. That is why ReLU can untangle a ring in one piece, while a smooth activation needs more width or depth to do the same work.

Precision adds a twist. A smooth tanh is only a faithful homeomorphism at enough numerical resolution. At low precision it saturates and quantizes into a staircase that maps many inputs to the same output. That many-to-one collapse is the same kind of move as a ReLU fold, which is why reduced-precision activations can sometimes make a hard problem easier instead of harder. The gain is not free: the same collapse throws information away. Low-precision polynomial approximations can match or beat full-precision baselines at the same accuracy budget (Zhang et al., 2024), but the effect depends on the task and the approximation.
