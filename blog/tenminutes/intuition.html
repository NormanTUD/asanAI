One word at a time.

A large language model is a next-token predictor. Chat, code, translation, reasoning — everything else is that one trick repeated in a loop, at enormous scale. Follow a sentence through the loop and see why next-token prediction can look like understanding.

01 The idea

The only trick it knows.

There is no “understanding” module inside a language model, no grammar checker, no shelf of stored facts. The model reads a sequence of tokens and returns a probability for every possible next token. One token is chosen, appended to the input, and the whole thing runs again.

That is the autoregressive loop: output becomes input, one step later. A conversation, a poem, a Python function — all of it is this loop, writing one piece at a time.

The aha

It outputs a distribution, not a single answer.

A decoder turns that distribution into text: greedy decoding always takes the top token, while temperature and top-p add controlled randomness. Novelty is not the same as creativity — and randomness alone usually produces noise, not insight.

Toy logits, not a real model. The mechanism is real: a distribution over the vocabulary, a decoding rule, append, repeat. This demo lets you switch between greedy, sampling, and top-p; production systems may also use beam search. Try temperature 0.2, then 1.8.

Temperature is only one decoding choice, and it does not change what the model knows. At temperature zero — greedy decoding — the top candidate is taken every time: deterministic, often repetitive, but still a freshly computed distribution rather than a lookup. Above zero, sampling draws from the distribution. Low temperature concentrates on likely tokens; high temperature flattens the distribution and can produce incoherence. Top-k and top-p restrict the pool before drawing.

The decoder in the demo is a choice, not a property of the model. Greedy always takes the top token; sampling draws from the whole distribution; top-p first keeps the smallest set of tokens whose probabilities reach 0.9 and then samples among them. The competence lives in the learned distribution; the decoder only selects from it. Variation is not creativity: useful novelty depends on structure, context, and the quality of the distribution, and raising the temperature usually trades coherence for surprise.

The aha

Prediction and compression are the same goal.

To predict the next token well, a model must find the shortest description of the data — and the shortest description of language keeps requiring a model of the world that produced it.

03 Inside the model

Six steps, one running vector.

The loop is the outside view. Inside, one token passes through a fixed pipeline. Each step changes the representation in a different way, and each is a small idea you can play with. The steps follow the order of a real transformer pass.

Inside the model · demos 02–07

Follow one token through six steps. Each step is a small toy, but the order and the division of labour are the real architecture.

six steps
Step 1 · Tokenize

Text becomes a sequence of ids

A model does not read words. It reads tokens: common words, word fragments, punctuation, and whitespace, each with a fixed numeric id. Rare words split into pieces the vocabulary already knows.

Step 2 · Embed

Ids become points in meaning space

The space has two semantic axes in this toy: power and gender. Change the expression or the highlighted words.

Meaning becomes geometry. Similar words sit close together, and a direction can carry a relationship: the arrow from man to woman is the same arrow as king to queen. Real embeddings use thousands of dimensions; this toy uses two so you can see the idea.

Step 3 · Position

Every slot gets its own fingerprint

Each row is one position. Each column is one dimension of the position signal.

Attention has no built-in sense of order. A positional encoding adds a unique fingerprint to every position, so “dog bites man” and “man bites dog” stop looking like the same bag of vectors.

Step 4 · Attend

Context pulls the word into place

I walk to the bank.
No context: bank stays in the middle, ambiguous.

An attention head compares a token with the other tokens and moves its representation toward the evidence. The same word can end up in a different place depending on the company it keeps.

The whole layer

The nonlinearity bends the space

The FFN above works on one token. A dense layer computes an affine projection, Ax + b, which moves the data into another vector space. The projection can rotate, scale, shear, or collapse dimensions, but on its own it maps straight lines to straight lines. The nonlinearity is what bends or folds the new space. A smooth activation produces the egg-shaped surface below; ReLU is the sharp version, a crease that maps every negative value to zero. The cut still happens — the layer changes the space in which the cut is made.

Step 6 · Residual

Every layer adds to the same stream

running stream

Every layer reads the same running vector, computes an update, and adds it back. Nothing is overwritten. The stream is a shared notebook that carries what the sentence means so far.

TakeawayTokens become vectors, positions add order, attention mixes context, the feed-forward network computes, and the residual stream carries everything forward.
The steps are deliberately small models. The real pipeline uses thousands of dimensions, many heads, and dozens of layers. The order and the division of labour stay the same.
04 The geometry

A finite vocabulary over an infinite space.

Under the hood, the model does not choose from a list. It arrives at a point in a continuous space of possible next-thoughts. Every word in the vocabulary owns the region of that space closest to it — like pins in a meadow, each with its own territory. The thought falls into the cell of the nearest word.

The boundaries between cells are seams: places where two words are equally near and the choice could tip either way. Move the thought around and watch the model become certain, then undecided, then certain again.

05 The whole loop

Text in, geometry, one word out.

Put the pieces in order and the machine is almost disappointingly simple: text is cut into tokens; each token becomes a vector; a position signal is added; layer after layer, attention lets every vector look at its neighbours while a residual stream carries earlier findings forward; the final vector is scored against the vocabulary and turned into a distribution; one token is drawn and appended.

No step stores a fact; no step “understands” in the human sense. Meaning lives in the geometry — directions, distances, regions — and the model’s only job at each step is to place the next thought somewhere in that space. The meadow is that last step, seen up close.

What it isA next-token predictor: one distribution, one draw, one append, repeated.
Why it scalesPrediction is compression; better compression needs more of the world’s structure.
Its limitsIt predicts plausible text. It can be fluent and wrong, and it offers no guarantee of truth.
The contested partWhether fluent prediction is genuine world knowledge or “stochastic parroting” is not settled.

That last point matters. The compression-as-intelligence view is a productive working hypothesis, not a proved identity. Solomonoff’s theorem is about prediction, not understanding; it does not settle whether a particular model has world knowledge. The honest position is that the empirical evidence is still catching up to the philosophy — and that both the awe and the scepticism are responses to the same remarkable fact: a loop this simple gets this far.
