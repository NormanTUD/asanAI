One word at a time.

A language model predicts the next word from the text so far. That one loop is the engine behind chat, code, translation, and reasoning. Start here: the rest of the calendar is machinery for making this single prediction better.

01 The idea

A first intuition: predict the next word.

Here is the core idea. A language model reads a sequence of tokens (words, in this toy) and returns a probability for every possible next token. One is chosen, appended to the input, and the whole thing runs again. That is the autoregressive loop.

Two words in that description are doing quiet work. “Probability” means the model ranks all candidates at once; it does not know the answer and then assign a score. “Chosen” is a separate decision, called decoding: the model supplies the distribution, and the decoder picks from it. The toy calls its tokens words; in a real model a token can be a whole word, a word fragment, or punctuation, which is the subject of tokenization.

This picture is deliberately incomplete. A real model adds layers of attention, a feed-forward network, position information, training data, and post-training. The loop comes first because everything else is machinery for making this one prediction better: each part exists to solve a problem the previous part created.

A conversation, a poem, a Python function: all of it is this loop. That sounds reductive until you notice what the context can contain: the question, a worked example, a tool's output, or the model's own earlier reasoning. Each new token conditions on all of it. Why not predict the whole answer at once? Because the model outputs a distribution over the next slot; the loop lets every word respond to the words already written.

The aha

At its heart, it is a loop: one word after another.

Pick a likely next word, append it to the input, and run the whole thing again. A sentence, an answer, or a program emerges from that repeated step.

Temperature is only one decoding choice, and it changes style, not knowledge. At temperature zero (greedy decoding) the top candidate is taken every time: deterministic, often repetitive, but still a freshly computed distribution rather than a lookup. Sampling draws from the distribution; top-p keeps the smallest set of words whose probabilities reach 0.9 and samples among them. The competence lives in the learned distribution, so if the model does not know a fact, no decoder setting makes it appear.

The aha

Prediction rewards compression.

A model that predicts the next word well has found a short description of the data. Whether that short description amounts to understanding the world is a live question, not a settled fact.

03 Inside the model

Six steps, one running vector.

The loop is the outside view. Inside, one word passes through a fixed pipeline. Each step changes the representation in a different way, and each is a small idea you can play with. The steps follow the order of a real transformer pass (Vaswani et al., 2017): text becomes ids, ids become vectors, position adds order, attention adds context, the feed-forward network computes, and the residual stream carries the result forward.

Step 1 · Tokenize

Text becomes a sequence of ids

A model does not read words. It reads tokens: common words, word fragments, punctuation, and whitespace, each with a fixed numeric id. The words in the toy above are a useful stand-in; rare words split into pieces the vocabulary already knows.

Step 2 · Embed

Ids become points in meaning space

The space has two semantic axes in this toy: power and gender. Change the expression or the highlighted words.

Meaning becomes geometry (Mikolov et al., 2013). Similar words sit close together, and a direction can carry a relationship: the arrow from man to woman is the same arrow as king to queen. Use the gender and power buttons to see each direction on its own; the arrows are labelled directly. A “dimension” is just one number in the vector; the axes here are labelled for humans, while a real model's axes are unlabelled directions that no single word owns. Real embeddings use thousands of dimensions; this toy uses two so you can see the idea.

Step 3 · Position

Every slot gets its own fingerprint

Each row is one position; each column is one dimension. Drag the context length to 128 and watch the low-frequency waves repeat while the high-frequency ones keep moving.

Attention has no built-in sense of order. A positional encoding adds a unique fingerprint to every position, so “dog bites man” and “man bites dog” stop looking like the same bag of vectors. Why not simply number the positions? Because a raw index grows without bound and says nothing about relative distance; a pattern of waves gives every position a distinct fingerprint and makes nearby positions share some structure.

Step 4 · Attend

Context pulls the word into place

No context: bank stays in the middle, ambiguous.

This step is an intuition, not a literal simulation. A real attention head computes query, key, and value vectors, turns their dot products into weights with a softmax, and sums the value vectors. It does not literally pick up the token's own embedding and slide it across the page. The moving dot is a teaching picture of the result (context changes the representation) with one hand-tuned head instead of the many heads and layers of a real model. For the full mechanism, see the attention lesson.

The query/key/value language is worth demystifying: the query is what a token is looking for, the key is what each token advertises, and the value is what it passes on when the match is good. Attention is a soft lookup, not a database. The same word can end up in a different place depending on the company it keeps, because the values it collects are different.

Step 6 · Residual

Every layer adds to the same stream

running stream

Every layer reads the same running vector, computes an update, and adds it back. Nothing is overwritten. The stream is a shared notebook that carries what the sentence means so far. Addition rather than replacement is not a detail: the original signal survives, and gradients have a short path back through the sum, which is a large part of why very deep networks train at all.

TakeawayTokens become vectors, positions add order, attention mixes context, the feed-forward network computes, and the residual stream carries everything forward.
The steps are deliberately small models. The real pipeline uses thousands of dimensions, many heads, and dozens of layers. The order and the division of labour stay the same.
04 The geometry

A finite vocabulary over an infinite space.

Under the hood, the model does not choose from a list. It arrives at a point in a continuous space of possible next-thoughts. Every word in the vocabulary owns the region of that space closest to it, like pins in a meadow, each with its own territory. The thought falls into the cell of the nearest word.

This is a visualization, not the literal algorithm. A real model produces one score, called a logit, for each vocabulary item, and a softmax turns those scores into probabilities. The meadow is a way to see why nearby meanings compete; it is not a claim that the model stores a map with pins. What is real is the consequence: the model can be deep inside one region, or standing on a boundary where two continuations are almost equally likely.

The boundaries between cells are seams: places where two words are equally near and the choice could tip either way. Move the thought around and watch the model become certain, then undecided, then certain again.

05 The whole loop

Text in, geometry, one word out.

Put the pieces in order and the outline is simple. The real model is much larger, and the remaining pages fill in the details. Text is cut into tokens. Each token becomes a vector. A position signal is added. Layer after layer, attention lets every vector look at its neighbours while a residual stream carries earlier findings forward. The final vector is scored against the vocabulary and turned into a distribution; one token is drawn and appended.

No step has a tidy fact database, and no step “understands” in the human sense. Knowledge is distributed across the weights; meaning lives in the geometry (directions, distances, regions) and at each step the model places the next thought somewhere in that space. The meadow is that last step, seen up close.

So where is a fact, if not in a table? Mostly in the feed-forward weights, and often superposed (Elhage et al., 2022): many features share the same directions rather than each owning a private neuron. That is why a fact can be partly present, why a model can know something without being able to say it reliably, and why editing one fact can disturb another. Fact lookup follows a single fact through the forward pass.

What it isA next-token predictor: one distribution, one draw, one append, repeated.
Why it scalesBetter prediction usually means better compression, and compression needs more of the world’s structure.
Its limitsIt predicts plausible text. It can be fluent and wrong, and it offers no guarantee of truth.
The contested partWhether fluent prediction is genuine world knowledge or “stochastic parroting” is not settled.

Compression-as-intelligence is a productive working hypothesis, not a proved identity. Solomonoff’s theorem is about prediction, not understanding; it does not settle whether a particular model grasps the world. The honest position is that the evidence is still catching up to the philosophy, and that both the awe and the scepticism are responses to the same remarkable fact: a loop this simple gets this far. Put differently, a language model is a finite vocabulary laid over a continuum of meaning, with a loop that keeps falling to the next word.
