The myths say more about us.

When a tool is opaque, we fill the gap with old stories: it is alive, a god, a threat, magic, or nothing. AI myths are modern legends, and they follow familiar patterns.

01 Reading the story, not just the claim

The Brednich method.

Folklorist Rolf Wilhelm Brednich studied modern legends. Almost every such story is false, most contain a spark of truth, and a few are true. The stories spread because they carry a fear, a taboo, a grudge, or a wish.

AI myths work the same way. 'It is alive', 'it thinks like us', 'the brain is a computer', 'it will outgrow us' are not only claims about machines; they are claims about what we hope and fear.

02 A better way to argue

Separate the fear from the claim.

Ask where a claim comes from, what evidence would change your mind, and what social need the story serves. A myth can contain a real concern — loss of control, loss of work, loss of truth — even when its literal claim is false.

The goal is not to debunk every story. It is to separate the technical question from the human one, and to keep both in view.

Media loopRepetition sharpens the story and hides the evidence.
“It is alive.” Projection is old. A fluent interface invites us to read a mind into the machine.

There is no evidence of experience. The useful question is not whether it feels alive, but what it can do and who is accountable.

“It thinks like us.” Human-like language is not human-like understanding.

The model learns statistical relationships, not a human world. Fluency is a clue, not proof of shared experience.

“It will outgrow us.” The singularity story is a modern legend with a real fear inside it.

Take the concern about control seriously without accepting the literal story. The question is governance, incentives, and oversight — not a movie plot.
