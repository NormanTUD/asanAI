01 The arc

Six movements, one machine.

The previous pages were the doors. This page is the map. Start with the engine: given a context, predict a distribution over the next token. Everything else is a choice about how to represent that context, how to compute with it, and how to turn a distribution into a sequence.

The compact course follows that dependency chain. The six movements are not six separate subjects; they are six questions asked in order. What is the prediction? How does the predictor learn? 03 The synthesis

How the pieces fit.

The engine. A language model does one thing: given a context, it produces a distribution over the next token. Training makes that distribution better at predicting text; decoding turns it into a sequence; post-training turns the sequence into an assistant. Everything else is a choice about representation, computation, data, or control.

The representation stack. Text is cut into tokens. Tokens become vectors. Position adds order. Attention mixes information across positions. The residual stream (Elhage et al., 2021) — the same running vector from the six-step demo, and the workspace every layer reads and adds to — carries a running state. The MLP, the feed-forward network inside each block, transforms each position on its own.

A transformer block repeats attention and MLP; the final vector is unembedded, multiplied against the vocabulary, into a distribution. The same geometry appears in embeddings, retrieval, and the residual stream. This is why the course keeps returning to vectors: the architecture is a machine for moving and reshaping points in one high-dimensional space.

The behaviour stack. Pretraining data decides what the model has seen. The objective — the loss it is trained to minimise — decides what it optimises. Post-training (Ouyang et al., 2022) decides how it behaves as an assistant. Sampling, the rule for turning probabilities into a chosen token, decides how adventurous it is. Retrieval (Lewis et al., 2020) and tools decide what it can use beyond its weights. None of these layers is the whole model; the assistant is the pipeline. Ask why two systems with the same base model behave differently, and the answer is usually somewhere in this stack, not in the architecture diagram.

The human stack. Evaluation asks whether it works. Safety asks what can go wrong. Society, philosophy, and the people behind the machine ask what it is for and who is accountable. A model is a stack of choices, not a single trick — and the choices at one layer change what the next layer can do.
