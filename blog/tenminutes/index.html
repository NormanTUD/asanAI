Lesson I · An intuition of how LLMs work~10 min

A machine that finishes your sentence.

A large language model is a next-word prediction machine. Given a context, it assigns a probability to every possible next token, samples one, appends it, and repeats. That is the whole loop. Chat, code, translation, and summary are that loop, repeated at enormous scale.

We can think of LLMs as machines that predict the next most likely word, given a context — only one word at a time. That word is appended to the input and fed back in, and the loop repeats until a special stop token signals the end.

In the example below, the user enters “Once upon a” and lets the model continue. Step 1: the text is put into the LLM. Step 2: the predicted word is attached, and the whole new text is fed back in. Step 3: it repeats — each answer becomes part of the next question — until the model emits the stop signal.

The five steps

1 Tokenize → 2 Embed → 3 Add position → 4 Transformer layers (attention + feed-forward) → 5 Score every token and sample one. Then loop.

The next lessons open each step. Here, stay with the loop: one local decision, repeated, until the sentence is finished.

The autoregressive loop

Click a candidate, or let it choose. The output becomes the next input.

What you are seeing: a hand-authored toy distribution, not a real LLM. The principle is the same: each step is a probability over the vocabulary, and the chosen token becomes context for the next step.

Why it can feel planned: a story is a narrow tube of plausible continuations. A model can stay inside it by picking one plausible step at a time; it can also build a latent plan that makes later steps easier. This toy shows the first mechanism. Do not mistake the simplicity of the loop for the simplicity of the model.

The aha

It writes by reading itself.

Every word it produces is appended to its own input and becomes the context for the next — the output is the input, one step later. But a single-step loop does not rule out planning: recent interpretability work has found evidence that models can form latent plans which shape many later tokens. The loop is the substrate, not the whole story.

How does a string of words become a probability? The LLM learned to do this by reading massive amounts of text — books, websites, articles — noticing the patterns in how language works and picking up facts along the way. The journey from a string of words to a single prediction happens in five steps. We can follow one word through all of them.

Step 1 — Tokenization. Computers don't understand words; they understand numbers. The first thing an LLM does is chop the input into tokens. They can be words, sub-words, numbers, or any part of a text. “Understanding” might split into under + standing — two pieces the model already knows. That is the quiet genius: a word the model has never seen can be assembled from pieces it has. The vocabulary is fixed for the life of the model, though, and a token that was rarely seen in training can behave like a blind spot.

Step 2 — Embedding. A raw token tells the model nothing about meaning. So the model replaces each token with a long list of numbers — a vector. These vectors live in a high-dimensional space where words with similar meanings end up close together. The space also captures relationships: the direction from man to woman is roughly the same arrow as from king to queen. Nobody hand-designs these positions. They fall out of the statistics of the text. Real networks use thousands of dimensions, far too many to picture; the principle stays the same: position encodes meaning, direction encodes relationship.

Step 3 — Positional encoding. “The dog bites man” means something very different from “The man bites dog.” Same words, different order. Attention and embedding have no built-in notion of sequence, so a positional signal is added to each token's vector: “I'm the 1st word,” “I'm the 2nd word,” and so on. Now each token carries two pieces of information: what it is and where it is.

Step 4 — The transformer layers. The vectors flow through many stacked layers. Each layer reads the running representation — the residual stream — thinks for a moment, and adds its finding back. Attention lets a token look at other tokens: in “The cat sat on the mat because it was tired,” an attention head can connect “it” to “cat.” It also moves a word like “bank” toward the river sense or the money sense depending on context. Many heads run in parallel. The feed-forward network then works on each token alone; in a modern LLM it holds about two-thirds of the parameters, which is why most stored knowledge lives there. A word like “Apple” meets different keys depending on whether the context is eating or buying, and writes back a different distribution: pie and juice, or Mac and iPhone.

Step 5 — The final prediction. After all the layers, the model takes the last token's vector and produces a score for every word in its vocabulary — often 50,000 or more. Those scores become probabilities. Then it picks a word, usually one of the top candidates, but with a little randomness, so it does not say the exact same thing every time. That word is appended to the input and the whole process begins again, one word at a time, until the model decides it is done. The model does not know the answer; it assigns probabilities and samples. Temperature is the knob that makes it greedy or adventurous.

The key insight. There is no “understanding” module, no grammar checker, no knowledge database. It is vectors flowing through layers of simple math: addition, multiplication, comparison. Stack enough of these operations together and something that looks a lot like understanding emerges. The deep connection is between prediction and compression: a model that predicts text well has found a compact description of its statistical structure. To compress language well, it helps to model the causal structure of the world that produced it. That is a powerful working hypothesis, not a proved identity. Whether a particular model has genuine world knowledge — or only fluent surface pattern-matching — remains contested.

The aha

Prediction and compression are the same goal.

To predict the next word well, a model has to find the shortest description of the data — and the shortest description of language keeps requiring a model of the world that produced it. Push prediction far enough and it presses into understanding. But do not overstate it: the loop is not a guarantee of truth, and planning is an emergent capacity, not a line of code.

The takeawayA language model is a next-token predictor in a loop. Chat is what that loop looks like when the context is a conversation. The loop is simple; the behavior is not.

You can now walk the five steps from text to next token — and say why “just predicting the next word” is both a modest and a surprisingly deep description.

Lesson II · A brief history of AI~10 min

The long line that led here.

The history of AI is not a straight line leading inevitably to the large language model. It is a convergence of ideas from unrelated fields — counting, logic, biology, war, games — that were never intended to meet.

The standard story runs through a handful of names: McCulloch and Pitts, Rosenblatt, backpropagation, the Transformer. The fuller story is a web. Statistical methods developed by astronomers to map the stars now weigh the next word in a sentence. GPU architectures designed for video games provide the parallel arithmetic that neural networks need. None of these fields set out to build a language model.

The line is older and stranger than the usual timeline: tally bones, Babylonian place value, zero, Aristotle’s syllogism, Pāṇini’s grammar, Llull’s rotating wheels, Leibniz’s dream of calculation, Babbage and Lovelace, Boole’s algebra of reason. The machine is a convergence, not a lightning strike.

What to notice

Every stop is a prerequisite, not a prophecy. The through-line is abstraction: each layer lets us stop doing the work and start managing the mechanism that does it.

1943-1969: the first spring. McCulloch and Pitts model the neuron as an all-or-none threshold unit; Hebb proposes that learning changes synaptic strength; Rosenblatt’s Perceptron learns from its errors and the Navy press conference promises a machine that will walk, talk, and be conscious. The limits are real: a single-layer threshold unit cannot compute XOR, and in 1969 Minsky and Papert prove it.

1973-1986: winter and return. The Lighthill report helps cut UK funding; ALPAC and the Mansfield Amendment had already shifted US money. The field retreats toward symbolic AI and expert systems. Then Rumelhart, Hinton, and Williams popularize backpropagation in 1986 — building on earlier work by Linnainmaa and Werbos — and hidden layers become trainable.

1989-2012: the long build. LeNet reads handwritten digits; LSTM learns to carry information across time; convolutional networks and recurrent networks mature. In 2012 AlexNet, two GPUs and 60 million parameters, wins ImageNet. The hardware lottery has paid out: the GPU was built for games, and it happens to be perfect for matrix multiplication.

2014-2022: attention and scale. Bahdanau and colleagues let a decoder look back at every encoder state. In 2017 the Transformer removes recurrence altogether. GPT-3 shows that scale turns a next-token predictor into a few-shot learner; Chinchilla shows that data and parameters must grow together; ChatGPT makes the loop feel like a conversation. The ideas were old. The scale was new.

More accurate: the book proved a limit of single-layer threshold units — including XOR — and said openly that multi-layer networks could escape it. The practical problem was that no one yet had a reliable way to train those hidden layers at scale.

The first winter had plural causes: the 1966 ALPAC report on machine translation, the 1969 Mansfield Amendment restricting US defense funding for open-ended research, and the UK’s 1973 Lighthill report, which helped cut AI funding in most British universities. The technical critique rode alongside the money; it did not act alone.

The lesson is not that one book froze a field. It is that funding, benchmarks, institutional fashion, and real technical limits move together — a pattern worth remembering the next time someone promises a straight line to the future.

The takeawayModern AI is not a lightning strike. It is a pile of ordinary inventions, each one making the next one thinkable.

Lesson III · The untold history of AI~10 min

The displaced prerequisites.

The main history follows the ideas: perceptrons, backpropagation, transformers. This history follows the other line — the discoveries made in fields that had no intention of producing a computer, from the chemistry that purified silicon to the looms that taught machines to read punched cards.

Finishing this project is impossible. Every discipline opens onto another, and a complete account would be the history of the world. The limit is deliberate: follow only the threads that can be shown to end, however indirectly, in a machine that learns. It is a selection, not an inventory.

The point is not that everything is connected to AI. The point is that capability is built from prerequisites that were not designed as prerequisites. Stars made the elements; geology made the atmosphere; life made oxygen; writing made external memory; printing made mass literacy; the loom made programmable input; war and spaceflight made the first electronic computers; games made the GPU.

01 · Stars and elements

The first minutes of the universe made mostly hydrogen and helium. Carbon, oxygen, silicon, and iron were forged inside stars and scattered by supernovae; much of the gold and platinum came from neutron-star mergers. Every atom in a chip was made in a star or in the violence that followed one.

The silicon in a wafer is, quite literally, star stuff that cooled into rock.

Thread to follow: 13.8 billion years → 4.6 billion → 3.8 billion → 2.4 billion → 3.3 million → 43,000 → 3200 BCE → 1450 → 1804 → 1947 → today.

Spacing compressed.

Why this belongs in the history of AI. A language model is not only a stack of mathematics. It is a stack of materials, institutions, and accidents: the glass that made lenses, the metals that carried current, the paper and printing that made mass literacy, the loom that separated instructions from material, the state that funded the first computers, the Web that became the training corpus, and the human labelers who turn raw text into useful data.

What the standard story leaves out. The usual timeline is a line of ideas. The full timeline is a supply chain and a society. Neither is optional. The ideas could not run without the materials, and the materials would not matter without the ideas.

The takeawayModern AI is a convergence of displaced prerequisites. The machine was assembled by the world long before anyone called it AI.

Lesson IV · Tokenization: how text becomes numbers~10 min

Text is not made of words.

Before a model can calculate anything, it chops text into pieces called tokens. Depending on how it chops, it sees the world differently. The tokenizer is not a neutral preprocessing step; it is a decision with consequences for cost, context, fairness, and failure.

Word-level is the intuitive choice: split on spaces and punctuation. It fails at the vocabulary wall. A word outside the list becomes unknown, and the model’s memory of it is thrown away. Treating “brave” and “bravely” as unrelated IDs is wasteful, too.

Character-level never hits the wall, but a sentence becomes five to ten times longer, and the dependencies the model must learn stretch further. N-grams cut at fixed lengths; they are consistent but destroy word meaning. The resolution is the subword: pieces between word and letter.

Byte-Pair Encoding began in 1994 as a data-compression algorithm. Start with bytes or characters, repeatedly merge the most frequent adjacent pair, and give the new symbol the next ID. Frequent patterns become short IDs; rare material stays a longer composition. In 2016 Sennrich and colleagues repurposed the same merging procedure to build a tokenizer. The compression algorithm became a segmentation algorithm, and the out-of-vocabulary wall collapsed.

The quiet failures

A token is not a concept. A string can become one token during tokenizer training and almost never appear during model training — a glitch token with a barely trained embedding. Numbers can split against their place value. A leading space can be its own token. Non-Latin scripts often cost more tokens for the same meaning.

The tokenizer’s training corpus and the model’s training corpus are not the same corpus. That mismatch is the root of several strange behaviors.

For curious minds

The model never sees letters. It sees the pieces the tokenizer decided to keep.

How would you chop it?

A real tokenizer is fixed before training. The same sentence can be cheap in one language and expensive in another — and the model’s context window is measured in tokens, not words.

WordPiece and byte-level BPE. WordPiece, the BERT tokenizer, merges pairs by a likelihood criterion rather than raw frequency and marks continuations with ##. GPT-style models use byte-level BPE: the alphabet is all 256 bytes, so every possible input is representable. That removes the “unknown token” failure, but it does not remove token inflation — a script with more bytes per character still costs more tokens.

Why it matters. Tokens are billed, counted against the context window, and attended to. A language that needs more tokens per word gets less effective context and a higher price for the same meaning. A vocabulary entry is not a learned concept; a token the model rarely saw during pretraining can behave like a blind spot. Test the tokenizer, not just the model.

The takeawayTokenization decides what the model can see cheaply. It is a compression scheme, a bias, and an economic decision — not a detail.

Lesson V · Embeddings: the geometry of meaning~10 min

A word is not a definition. It is a location.

In a transformer, a word has no intrinsic soul or static dictionary definition. Its identity is defined by its context — its use. That philosophical principle is operationalized through a high-dimensional embedding space, where concepts become coordinates in a continuous geometric manifold.

The theoretical root is older than the computer. Saussure saw language as a system of differences: a word means what it means because it is not the other words. Firth gave the idea its slogan in 1957: “You shall know a word by the company it keeps.” Distributional semantics turned that intuition into vectors; word2vec made the arithmetic famous in 2013.

Before meaning, there is a token: a chunk of text. A tokenizer outputs integers; the first thing a transformer does is look each ID up in a trainable embedding matrix, one row per token. Everything after that happens in vector space. Similar contexts pull words into the same neighborhood; relationships become directions you can travel.

The one-line idea

Meaning is relational. A word is defined by the company it keeps — not by a definition stored in a dictionary.

Word2vec (2013) made the arithmetic famous; GloVe (2014) used global co-occurrence; fastText (2016) added character n-grams. ELMo (2018) and BERT (2018) made the vector depend on the whole sentence.

The famous analogy, carefully. In some embedding spaces, king − man + woman lands near queen, and the same “gender” arrow reappears between uncle and aunt. This is a tendency, not a theorem. It depends on the corpus and the training objective, and the same geometry can encode stereotypes. The interesting fact is not that the arithmetic is perfect; it is that no one designed it. The structure emerges from prediction.

From static to contextual. Word2vec, GloVe, and fastText give each word one vector. ELMo and BERT give each occurrence a vector, so “bank” in “river bank” and “bank account” can move apart. The embedding table at the input of a modern LLM is still a descendant of word2vec, but context rewrites it immediately.

The manifold hypothesis. Real data does not fill a 1,536-dimensional cube uniformly. It lies on or near a much lower-dimensional surface — a manifold — folded into the larger space. That is why a 2-D sketch can be useful, and why it is also a distortion. The map is not the territory.

The takeawayMeaning is a position and a direction. That is why the model can generalize from “king” to “queen” without a rule that says so — and why the geometry must be checked, not worshipped.

Lesson VI · Neural networks: learning by being wrong~10 min

The machine improves by measuring its own error.

The simplest AI is a very simple mathematical model called a neuron: an affine map, a line with a slope and a shift. Training is the loop that finds the slope and shift — and, once you stack layers with a nonlinearity, almost any shape.

1943: the logical neuron. McCulloch and Pitts model a neuron as an all-or-none threshold unit: a weighted sum is compared with a threshold, and the unit fires when the sum crosses it. The paper shows that networks of such units can realize any propositional formula — but it contains no rule for changing the weights.

1949-1958: learning from error. Hebb proposes that learning changes synaptic strength; the slogan “cells that fire together, wire together” is a later summary. Rosenblatt’s Perceptron adds an error-correction rule: change the weights only when the output is wrong. The Mark I used a 20×20 photocell retina and motor-driven potentiometers as weights. ADALINE soon replaced the step with a linear output and the delta rule — the gradient-descent update used in the lab below.

Training is the loop that finds the weights: predict, measure the loss, compute the gradient, take a small step downhill. Backpropagation is the chain rule applied backwards, so every weight learns how much it contributed to the error. Stacking linear layers alone collapses into one affine map; a nonlinearity between them is what lets the composition bend.

The loop

Guess → loss → gradient → step. Repeat a few million times and the knobs settle into a function that fits the data.

This is the same loop behind every modern network. The difference is scale: billions of knobs, trillions of tokens, and hardware built to multiply matrices in parallel.

VII
Lesson VII · Training: the shape of a run~10 min

Scale is a budget, not a button.

A training run is a phenomenon you can watch, not just a number you report. The loss falls as a power law, warms up, holds, decays, and sometimes spikes. Parameters and tokens trade off. Batch size sets the noise. Learning rate decides whether the run survives.

The power law. Kaplan and colleagues fit the held-out loss of language models as a sum of two power laws in parameters N and tokens D, plus an irreducible floor: L = E + A·N^−αN + B·D^−αD. Double the parameters or the data and the loss drops by a fixed, modest factor. That is why scale is a budget line, not a magic switch.

Chinchilla. Hoffmann and colleagues redid the fit at larger scale and found the exponents essentially equal. Compute is roughly 6ND, so for a fixed budget you can trade parameters against data. The optimum sits near twenty tokens per parameter. The field had been training models that were too large and too data-hungry; Chinchilla said: smaller model, more tokens.

The schedule. Warmup ramps the learning rate up over the first steps so Adam’s moment estimates can settle. Cosine decay moves fast early and precisely late. WSD holds a stable rate, then decays; it lets you extend training by unrolling the stable phase. Batch size sets the noise of the gradient, which scales as 1/√batch; the linear scaling rule says that when you double the batch, you can roughly double the learning rate.

Failure modes. A loss spike is a sudden jump; a healthy run recovers. Divergence does not. The edge of stability is the narrow regime where a high learning rate oscillates but still trends down. Gradient clipping keeps a high-rate run on the edge instead of past it. μP is the attempt to make tuned hyperparameters transfer from a tiny model to a huge one.

Read the curve

Where is it going? The power law sets the floor. How does it get there? The schedule. Is it going to make it? The spike — and whether it recovers.

A loss that oscillates but trends down is not necessarily broken. A loss that jumps and stays up is a lost run.

Lesson VIII · The transformer~10 min

Every token can look at every other token.

Attention is a learned routing table. Each token asks a question, every token advertises what it contains, and the matching content flows back. Position tells the model where it is; the feed-forward network transforms what it found; residual connections keep the signal alive.

Attention is the routing layer. A token makes a query; every token offers a key; the match decides how much of each value flows into the next representation. A causal mask hides the future, so the model can only use what it has already seen.

The feed-forward network then transforms each token on its own. Residual connections carry the original signal forward, and the block is stacked. At the end, the model scores every token in its vocabulary and samples one. Then it does it again.

Handle with care

Attention is not awareness. It is a weighted average, borrowed from the vocabulary of psychology but implemented as matrix multiplication.

A pretty attention map can be illustrative and still not be an explanation. Real heads mix many roles, and the map is only one layer of many.

The whole arc · 60 secondssynthesis

A predictor, not an oracle.

You now have the whole loop: text becomes tokens, tokens become geometry, attention routes context, the feed-forward network transforms, and the model predicts the next token. Training shaped every weight by error. Scale made it fluent.

What the loop does not give you is truth. A language model is optimized to produce plausible text, not to be a database or an oracle. It can be fluent and useful; it can also be confidently wrong. That is why retrieval, evaluation, and human judgment are part of any serious system.

You do not need to know the math to see the shape. You needed the loop, the history, and a feel for what the numbers are doing.

What it is / is not

Is: a statistical model of text; a next-token predictor; a compression of patterns; a tool that can be evaluated, grounded, and constrained.

Is not: a database; a search engine; a mind with intentions; an oracle; a guarantee of facts.

Nuance worth keeping

  • The first AI winter had multiple causes: ALPAC (1966), the Mansfield Amendment (1969), the Lighthill report (1973), and genuine technical limits. Minsky and Papert proved a limit of single-layer threshold units, not the impossibility of neural networks.
  • “king − man + woman ≈ queen” is an approximate tendency of some embedding spaces, popularized by word2vec. It is not a law, and embeddings can encode social biases.
  • Attention is a weighted routing mechanism, not awareness. Attention maps are illustrative; a head can mix several roles.
  • Chinchilla’s ~20 tokens per parameter is compute-optimal guidance under a particular setup, not a universal rule.
  • Big Bang nucleosynthesis made mostly hydrogen and helium. Heavier elements were forged later in stars and stellar mergers.
  • The Transformer was introduced for machine translation in 2017. ChatGPT arrived in late 2022, after scale, instruction tuning, and preference learning.
  • The autoregressive loop does not imply that a model never plans. Interpretability work — for example, Anthropic’s circuit tracing — has found evidence of forward planning: a model can commit to a rhyme or an intermediate answer several tokens before it appears. The loop is the substrate; planning is an emergent strategy on top of it.
