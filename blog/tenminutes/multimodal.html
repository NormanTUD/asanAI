Two worlds, one space.

A text model lives in a language space; a vision model lives in a visual space. Multimodal models learn to put them in the same room.

01 The alignment problem

Pull matching pairs together.

An image encoder turns a picture into a vector; a text encoder turns a caption into another vector. The two spaces are different, so you cannot compare them directly. Contrastive learning pulls matched image-caption pairs together and pushes mismatched pairs apart.

CLIP made this practical at scale. After alignment, a picture of a dog and the word 'dog' are close in a shared space, and the model can do zero-shot classification by comparing an image with candidate captions.

02 From alignment to conversation

Pixels become tokens.

A multimodal LLM goes further. Pixels are turned into visual tokens, projected into the language model's embedding space, and then processed together with text tokens. The model can answer questions about an image, describe it, or use it as context.

The alignment is not perfect. The model can miss small details, misread text in images, or inherit biases from its training data. Multimodality adds a new input, not a guarantee of understanding.

03 What alignment buys you

Zero-shot and cross-attention.

Once an image and a caption share a space, you can compare them. CLIP uses this for zero-shot classification: embed the image, embed a set of candidate captions, and choose the closest pair. No task-specific classifier is needed for every new label set.

A multimodal LLM goes further. Visual tokens are projected into the language model's space, and cross-attention lets the text query the image. The model can answer questions, describe a scene, or use the image as context for a longer conversation.

The alignment is statistical, not perfect. The model can miss small details, misread text in an image, or inherit biases from its training data. Multimodality adds a new input, not a guarantee of understanding.

Background

Multimodal LLMs: How a Text Model Learns to See

A pretrained vision encoder (e.g., CLIP-ViT) produces a sequence of visual tokens. A small projection MLP maps each visual token from the vision space into the LLM's text-token embedding space:

The projected visual tokens are concatenated with the text-token sequence and fed to the LLM as if they were ordinary words. Only the projection layer is trained initially; the LLM is frozen. The visual tokens literally become “foreign-language words” the LLM learns to read.

Background

How a Multimodal LLM Answers a Question

The LLM treats the visual tokens as if they were a foreign language it has been taught to read. No new attention mechanism is required, the same Q/K/V machinery already described in the Attention chapter handles the cross-modal mixing, because visual and text tokens occupy the same vector space.

Visual Question Answering Benchmarks

The leaderboard has shifted dramatically: in 2022, GPT-3.5 with no vision got ~0% on MMMU. By 2025, top models (GPT-4o, Claude 3.5, Gemini 1.5 Pro) exceed 70%, and on VQA v2 they exceed 85%.

Summary

The hard parts are data (curating trillions of matched image-text-audio samples, see the Training Data chapter), compute (vision towers + LLMs + alignment data = massive training runs), and evaluation (the benchmarks above are still being developed). But the architectural recipe is now standard and reproducible, which is why every frontier lab ships multimodal-by-default in 2025.

Practical notes

Alignment is not understanding.

Multimodal models can miss small details, misread text in images, or hallucinate an object that is not there. The shared space is statistical; it does not guarantee that the model saw what you saw.

Evaluation is hard. A model can score well on a benchmark and still fail on a new viewpoint, a rare object, or a misleading caption. Use the model as an assistant, not as an oracle.
