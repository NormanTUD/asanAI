Can a machine think?

The question is easy to ask and hard to make precise. Behaviour, understanding, meaning, and experience are different things, and a model can have some without the others.

01 Behaviour and presence

The Turing Test is not a definition.

Society asked what the machine does to us. This page asks what the machine is. The Turing Test (Turing, 1950) is a behavioural proposal: if a machine can fool a human in conversation, should we call it intelligent? In its original form it is the imitation game: a judge holds a text-only conversation with a human and a machine and tries to say which is which. Turing offered it as a way to sidestep a metaphysical question, not as a definition of mind.

A model can produce the right answer without the inner life we associate with thinking. The philosophical question is whether that distinction matters, and if so, how we could ever tell. It helps to separate intelligence — the capacity to solve problems, adapt, and generalise — from consciousness — the fact that there is something it is like to be the system. A thing can be intelligent without being conscious, and the reverse is at least conceivable.

02 Grounding and experience

Meaning and the hard problem.

Symbols in a computer are not connected to the world in the way human words are. The grounding problem asks how meaning gets attached to tokens. A model learns statistical relationships, but does it refer to anything? Searle's Chinese Room (Searle, 1980) makes the worry vivid: a person in a room follows rules for manipulating Chinese symbols and produces plausible answers without understanding Chinese. If the room passes the test, has it understood? The argument is contested — the whole room might be the understanding system — but it names the gap precisely.

Mary's Room (Jackson, 1982) and the hard problem of consciousness push on a different question: could a system know everything about colour and still never experience it? The hard problem (Chalmers, 1995) is why any physical process feels like something at all; the easy problems are about function and behaviour, and the hard one is about experience. We have no accepted test for experience. That uncertainty is part of the answer.

03 Experience and sentience

What would it be like?

Mary's Room is a thought experiment about knowledge and experience. Mary knows every physical fact about colour, but she has never seen red. When she finally does, does she learn something new? If yes, then knowing facts is not the same as having an experience.

The hard problem of consciousness asks why any physical process feels like something at all. A model can process the word 'red' without seeing red, and it can describe pain without feeling it. Behaviour does not settle the question of experience.

We have no accepted test for experience. That uncertainty is part of the answer. The ethical question is not only whether a model is conscious, but how we should treat systems whose status we cannot determine.

Background

The Philosophy of Computation: Is Simulation Sufficient?

A deeper question underlies the entire debate about AI understanding and consciousness: what is computation, and what can it achieve? This question has been explored since the 1930s, and the answers bear directly on the claims made about LLMs throughout this text.

The Church-Turing Thesis (1936) states that any function that can be effectively computed can be computed by a Turing machine. This is not a claim about what machines can do; it is a claim about what computation means. It defines the boundaries of the computable: anything that falls outside the reach of a Turing machine is, by definition, not computable. The thesis has been remarkably resilient, and no counterexample has been found. But the question for AI is not whether LLMs can compute (they obviously can), but whether computation is sufficient for understanding, consciousness, and meaning.

The Trolley Problem: Machines Making Moral Decisions

Philippa Foot introduced the Trolley Problem in 1967. A runaway trolley is barreling toward five workers on the track. You are standing next to a lever that can divert it to a side track, but there is one person on that side track. Pull the lever and one dies; do nothing and five die. What do you do?

For AI the trolley problem is less a decision procedure than a reminder that moral judgment is contextual. An autonomous vehicle cannot convene a committee at the moment of a crash; whatever values it has must be built into its policy in advance, and the choice of policy is a political act, not a neutral engineering default.

Three positions structure the disagreement. The utilitarian answer is arithmetic: five lives outweigh one life, so pull the lever. But the thought experiment has a twist built for exactly this move: what if the one person is your child? Utilitarianism says it should not matter, and the fact that it does matter to most people reveals that few of us are pure utilitarians; our moral intuitions resist cold calculation.

The deontologist answer (in Kant's tradition) refuses the lever: there is a moral difference between killing and letting die. By pulling the lever you become the cause of death. Inaction feels different from action, even when the outcome is worse — which is why doctors distinguish between “doing harm” and “allowing natural death.”

The virtue ethicist or care ethicist answer holds that context and relationships matter: a mother saving her child is not selfish but human, and morality cannot be reduced to abstract rules or arithmetic. The “right” thing depends on the web of relationships and circumstances you are embedded in.

More background

What is Reason?

Reasoning is the process, using more-or-less rational thought and cognition to extrapolate from existing knowledge and generate new knowledge. The field of logic studies the formal version: how to produce logically valid arguments and true conclusions.

Chain-of-thought prompting blends four modes of reasoning: it decomposes a problem (deductive), appeals to patterns seen in training (inductive), proposes candidate answers (abductive), and maps structure across domains (analogical).

A Taxonomy of Intelligence Tests and Challenges

The Winograd Schema is one node in a much larger web of attempts to test whether a machine is intelligent. A Winograd schema is a sentence pair where one word flips the referent of a pronoun: “The trophy would not fit in the suitcase because it was too big” versus “because it was too small.” Resolving “it” requires world knowledge, not just grammar, which is why the pair is hard to game with surface statistics. Each test isolates a different hypothesis about what intelligence is, and each has been met with a different kind of defeat. The pattern is a shift from form to content.

The old tests probed the form of thinking; the new ones probe its content. The Turing Test and the Chinese Room ask whether the right symbols move in the right ways. The benchmark generation — WSC, CLEVR, CommonSenseQA — asks whether the machine actually holds the world the symbols point at. This is a direct consequence of the shift from symbolic to statistical AI: once you stop hand-coding the knowledge, the knowledge itself becomes what you must test for.
