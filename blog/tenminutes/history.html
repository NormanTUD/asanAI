A very brief history of AI.

Modern AI grew out of counting bones, geared models of the sky, formal logic, cybernetic feedback, a machine that learned from its mistakes, two winters, and a crowd of contributors who are usually left out of the story. The route is longer and more collective than the usual parade of famous names.

01 The long fuse

Many currents met here.

Next-token prediction reduced the machine to a loop. This page asks where that loop came from. The history of AI has no single line to the large language model. It is a convergence of ideas from unrelated fields that were never intended to meet. Statistical methods developed by astronomers to map the stars — least squares was a way to fit noisy celestial observations — now weigh the next word in a sequence. GPU architectures designed for video games provide the parallel computation neural networks need: the matrix operations that draw polygons are the same ones that multiply activations. The convergence is not a plan; it is a series of borrowings that turned out to fit.

The deeper history is a history of abstraction: human effort drawn away from the direct task and handed to a mechanism. It begins roughly 3.3 million years ago with the first stone tools, continues through the wheel and Heron of Alexandria’s programmable cart, and crosses from the physical to the cognitive when thought itself becomes a formal calculus a machine can run. The claim is not that AI was inevitable; it is that the same move — hand a task to a mechanism, then treat the mechanism as the new primitive — recurs at every scale.

We can solve any problem by introducing an extra level of indirection. David Wheeler
The aha

The machine is the ultimate indirection.

The wheel abstracts movement; the computer abstracts thought. Both remove the human from the raw labour while keeping the human in charge of the mechanism.

03 External memory

Tools, marks, and memory.

Long before counting, there is technique. At Lomekwi 3 in West Turkana, Kenya, hominins were knapping stone by about 3.3 million years ago, before the genus Homo. The tools are coarse and simple, yet deliberate: someone chose a stone and struck flakes from it. They are the oldest known stone assemblage. The date comes from the stratigraphy and radiometric dating of the surrounding layers; the interpretation of the flakes as deliberately made, rather than naturally broken, is argued from the pattern of strikes.

Marks come later. A zig-zag incised into a river shell at Trinil, Java, is about 500,000 years old — the oldest known deliberate geometric incision. It is a pattern; it does not yet record a count, and whether it carried symbolic meaning is debated. What is clear is that the mark was made on purpose and kept.

At Blombos Cave in South Africa, pieces of ochre carry cross-hatched grids and rhomboid fields from roughly 75,000 years ago (Henshilwood et al.). The surface is divided into a regular network of cells: order made visible, though we cannot read its meaning.

Counting appears in bone. The Lebombo bone (c. 43,000–42,000 years ago) carries 29 notches. The Dolní Věstonice wolf bone (c. 27,000) carries 55 notches in two groups of 30 and 25. The Ishango bone (c. 18,000 BCE) carries grouped notches that may encode a tally, a lunar calendar, or something else; its prime-number column is suggestive, not settled. The calendar readings are interpretations, not decoded messages. The same impulse — outsource counting to a physical object — appears independently in Africa and Europe.

The Salamis Tablet (c. 300 BCE) is the oldest surviving counting board: a marble slab with etched lines and pebbles (calculi). It maps numbers to grid coordinates, uses a rule-based physical system for arithmetic, and stores intermediate sums outside the head. The word calculus comes from the pebbles.

The Antikythera mechanism (c. 200–60 BCE, recovered in 1901) takes the next step: at least 30 meshing bronze gears model the sky, predicting astronomical positions and eclipses decades in advance. For over a millennium, no equally complex geared technology is known in the West. Computation also has roots in mechanical simulation.

The aha

Memory can sit on a shelf.

A notch in a bone keeps a count beyond the moment of counting. Ledgers, catalogues and databases repeat the same move: they put the number somewhere you can come back to.

04 The rules of thought

Reason and speech become calculi.

The number systems that feed the modern machine grew out of several separate inventions: place value (Babylonian, c. 2000 BCE), zero as a number with arithmetic rules (Brahmagupta, 628 CE), negative quantities (Han-dynasty Chinese linear-equation texts), and decimal fractions (Stevin, 1585, building on earlier Arabic work). Babylonian place value was base 60, which is why an hour still has sixty minutes and a circle 360 degrees. Pingala’s Sanskrit prosody (c. 3rd–1st century BCE) uses a binary-like scheme for metres — the mapping to 0 and 1 is a later reading — and Leibniz later formalised binary notation.

Modern hardware inherits the positional idea, then makes its own engineering choices. A binary integer has a fixed width and a finite range. A floating-point number stores a sign, an exponent, and a significand, so it approximates real numbers with limited precision; the usual float value for 0.1 is not exactly one tenth. Most processors follow the IEEE 754 conventions for these formats.

In the same long arc, reasoning becomes a formal system. Aristotle’s syllogism separates the structure of an argument from its content. Pāṇini’s Aṣṭādhyāyī gives Sanskrit a fully generative grammar of metarules, transformations, and recursion — a compiler-like contract centuries before the computer. Ramon Llull’s Ars Magna (1305) turns concentric wheels of concepts to generate every logically possible combination: the first documented attempt to mechanise thought. George Boole’s algebra (1854) then makes logic calculable, and Frege and Russell spend the next decades trying to ground mathematics in it.

The aha

Logic becomes something you can turn.

A syllogism, a grammar, and a wheel of concepts are all attempts to make valid reasoning mechanical — long before there is a machine to run it.

05 Calculemus

Reason becomes arithmetic.

Leibniz built a calculating machine, the stepped reckoner, begun in 1673 and completed in 1694; it was the first calculator able to perform all four arithmetic operations. Behind the brass was a larger programme: a mathesis universalis, a notation in which every concept has a number and every argument becomes a calculation. He also formalised binary notation, reducing logic to 0 and 1. The machine itself was not reliable in practice — the carry mechanism gave trouble — but the design and the programme outlived the brass.

His hope was that disagreement could be settled by arithmetic rather than rhetoric. The invitation usually quoted is to sit down at the slate and calculate.

If controversies were to arise, there would be no more need of disputation between two philosophers than between two accountants. For it would suffice to take their pencils in their hands, to sit down at their slates, and to say to each other: Let us calculate. (Calculemus!) Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz
The aha

If reasoning can be written, it can be mechanised.

Leibniz left the thinking machine to later centuries. What he supplied was the wager that thought might be written down as calculation.

06 Engines and programs

The machine learns to follow a stored plan.

Babbage designs the Analytical Engine, with a store, a mill, and punched-card input. It was never completed in his lifetime; the design, not a working machine, is what survived. Ada Lovelace sees further than the machine’s builder: the Engine can manipulate symbols, not only numbers, and she writes the first published algorithm intended for it.

The 20th century turns the plan into hardware. Konrad Zuse’s Z3 (1941) is the first functional, programmable, fully automatic digital computer, built from 2,000 telephone relays; he also designs Plankalkül, an early high-level language. “First” depends on the definition: the Z3 was electromechanical, not electronic, and other machines were programmable in weaker senses.

Howard Aiken’s Harvard Mark I (1944) is a 50-foot decimal relay machine. Turing’s 1936 universal machine supplies the theory (Turing, 1936); his ACE and Bletchley work supply the practice. Shannon’s 1937 master’s thesis (Shannon, 1937) shows that Boolean algebra can be realised in relay circuits — the bridge from logic to electronics. Von Neumann’s stored-program design (1945) makes “reprogramming” a matter of software rather than rewiring.

The aha

The stored program separates the machine from its behaviour.

Once instructions live in the same memory as data, a computer can be changed by writing rather than by rebuilding. Software is born.

07 The neuron and the perceptron

A machine that learns from its mistakes.

The first logical neuron came from an unlikely pair. Warren McCulloch was a neuropsychiatrist, philosopher, and poet. He ran a lab under the sign “Experimental Epistemology” and asked, “What is a number, that a man may know it, and a man, that he may know a number?” Walter Pitts was a self-taught logician who had read Principia Mathematica at twelve, written to Bertrand Russell, and run away from home at fifteen to hear Russell lecture in Chicago. They met there, found a shared hero in Leibniz, and began to model the brain as a logical calculus.

Strictly speaking, the first formal neuron model was Louis Lapicque’s integrate-and-fire cell, published in the Journal de Physiologie et de Pathologie Générale in 1907 (Lapicque, 1907, p. 620). Lapicque treated the nerve membrane as a capacitor: input current charges it, the voltage leaks away, and when it crosses a threshold the cell fires and resets. It is a phenomenological model, not a detailed biophysical one, but it is the ancestor of the point-neuron models used in computational neuroscience (Abbott, 1999). McCulloch and Pitts built the first logical neuron, which is what most histories mean by “the first artificial neuron.”

Their 1943 paper, “A Logical Calculus of the Ideas Immanent in Nervous Activity,” appeared in the Bulletin of Mathematical Biophysics, not in a physiology journal. It made five assumptions: a neuron fires all-or-none; a fixed number of excitatory synapses must be active within the period of latent addition; the only significant delay is synaptic; an inhibitory synapse absolutely prevents firing; and the structure of the net does not change with time. The neuron fires when the excitatory input reaches its threshold and no inhibition is present. The model counts synapses. The familiar y = σ(Σ wᵢxᵢ − θ) belongs to the later perceptron; McCulloch and Pitts had no learned weights.

The paper works in both directions. Given a network, you can read off the propositions its activity realizes; given a logical expression, you can build a network that realizes it. Networks without loops compute Boolean functions. Networks with loops can hold a state, and McCulloch and Pitts argued that such a net, with a tape and scanners, can compute what a Turing machine computes. The loop was the hard part: a signal that circles back seems to make time run backwards. Pitts’s way out was clock arithmetic. Inside a loop, “before” and “after” lose their meaning; the signal records that an event happened, not when. McCulloch called the result “an idea wrenched out of time” — a memory.

The paper reached the hardware of the next decade. Von Neumann’s 1945 EDVAC report, the first published description of the stored-program computer, cited exactly one paper — this one — and proposed building the machine from McCulloch–Pitts-style logic gates, with Pitts’s loops as memory. At the 1948 Hixon Symposium, von Neumann gave the nets a wide audience and called their theorems “probably the most significant result obtained with the axiomatic method up to now.” Pitts, the runaway logician, became the hinge between McCulloch’s physiology and von Neumann’s computer.

The collaboration was also a friendship. McCulloch invited the homeless Pitts to live with his family; Pitts called the house home for the rest of his life. McCulloch called him his “bootlegged collaborator.” Both wrote poetry. Pitts struggled with depression, broke with Norbert Wiener, and died in 1969 at forty-six; McCulloch died four months later. The 1943 neuron outlived them both.

When an axon of cell A is near enough to excite a cell B and repeatedly or persistently takes part in firing it [...] some growth process or metabolic change takes place in one or both cells such that A’s efficiency, as one of the cells firing B, is increased.

Donald O. Hebb, The Organization of Behavior (1949), p. 62

The slogan is “neurons that fire together, wire together.” The SNARC (1951) was the first physical neural network. Then, in 1958, Frank Rosenblatt introduced the perceptron (Rosenblatt, 1958): a system that adjusted its weights in response to errors. The Mark I Perceptron was constructed between 1958 and 1960 and publicly demonstrated in 1960: a 20×20 grid of 400 photocells formed its retina, and electric motors turned the potentiometers that held the weights. Its follow-on, Tobermory, was designed for speech recognition.

The learning rule was not backpropagation: the perceptron updated its weights directly from the error, one layer at a time, which is why it could not learn the internal representations a deep network needs.

The press heard “a machine that will walk, talk, see, write, reproduce itself and be conscious of its existence.” The science heard a learning rule, Δw = η · (target − output) · input, that turns error into a weight change. Minsky and Papert’s Perceptrons (1969) then showed the limits of a single-layer network — famously, XOR — and the first winter began. The critique was about one layer; a multilayer network can represent XOR, and backpropagation later supplied a way to train one. The idea survived in the margins.

The bibliography was a giveaway

The 1943 paper cites Carnap’s Logical Syntax of Language, Hilbert and Ackermann’s Grundzüge der theoretischen Logik, and Russell and Whitehead’s Principia Mathematica. It cites no neurophysiologist. The physiology sits in the assumptions; the argument is in the logic.

Spotlight · 1928–1971

The perceptron was a physical machine.

Motor-driven potentiometers whose resistance was the weight, wired to photocells, nudged after every mistake. Rosenblatt’s work is the hinge between “a program does what it is told” and “a model learns from what it sees.” He died in a sailing accident in 1971, two years after the critique that helped cool the field.

The descendants of that machine are in every transformer today. The perceptron ran into the limits of one layer, limited data, and limited compute. Learning from data survived the disappointment.

Norbert Wiener came to the loop through war work at MIT, where he designed predictors for anti-aircraft fire. A gun cannot aim at where a plane is; it has to estimate where the plane will be. In Cybernetics (1948) Wiener turned that practical problem into a general language of feedback, control and communication, and argued that a thermostat, a nervous system and a computer could be described with the same vocabulary.

W. Ross Ashby built the loop as a machine. His Homeostat (1948) is a box of four magnetized units wired so that a disturbance changes their connections. The machine hunts through configurations until its output settles; it has no model of the world and no named goal. It simply restores its own equilibrium, an early physical model of adaptation and homeostasis.

Grey Walter built the loop as an animal. His tortoises, Elmer and Elsie, were small autonomous robots with a light sensor and a contact switch; they crawled toward light, backed away from obstacles, and recharged themselves. A handful of valves produced behaviour that looked purposeful.

Gregory Bateson, an anthropologist in the same conversations, gave the loop its most portable definition: information is “a difference that makes a difference.” The vocabulary of modern AI — feedback, adaptation, signal, noise — is largely this group’s inheritance.

The aha

Learning is error correction.

The perceptron does not need a rule for every case. It needs an error signal and a way to change its weights. That is the seed of every trained model.

08 Winters and the return

Promise, winter, quiet accumulation.

The Dartmouth workshop of 1956 names the field. Symbolic AI builds the Logic Theorist, GPS, expert systems, planners, and microworlds such as SHRDLU. Then it hits the frame problem and the limits of hand-coded rules. The frame problem is the difficulty of representing what does not change when an action is taken: a hand-coded system has to be told that moving a cup does not move the table, the room, or the weather, and the list of things that stay the same is endless. Funding freezes in the first AI winter; connectionism returns in the 1980s; a second winter follows. Deep Blue beats Garry Kasparov in 1997, a triumph of search and evaluation in a narrow, hand-tuned domain.

The winters were not one event. The first was driven by the 1969 Perceptrons critique, the failure of ambitious machine-translation projects, and the Lighthill report in Britain (1973), which questioned whether the field had delivered on its promises. The second followed the brittleness of expert systems and the collapse of the specialised Lisp-machine market. Different countries cut funding at different times; the pattern was a cycle, not a single verdict.

The return of neural networks came as a stack of changes: backpropagation, larger datasets, GPUs, better regularization. AlexNet wins ImageNet in 2012 (Krizhevsky et al., 2012). AlphaGo defeats Lee Sedol in 2016. In 2017, “Attention Is All You Need” replaces recurrence with self-attention, making the path between distant tokens one step and the computation parallel. ChatGPT (2022) turns the next-token loop into a public interface. The ideas are old; the scale, the data, and the engineering are new.

Geoffrey Hinton kept the connectionist bet alive through the winters and, with Alex Krizhevsky and Ilya Sutskever, built AlexNet — the 2012 network that made deep learning hard to ignore. The architecture mattered, but so did the engineering: training it on two consumer GPUs made a network that was previously impractical finish in days. He later shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Physics with John Hopfield.

Yann LeCun made convolutions work. LeNet read handwritten digits, first on ZIP codes for the U.S. Postal Service and later on bank checks.

Yoshua Bengio brought neural networks back to language with a 2003 neural probabilistic language model, replacing sparse n-gram tables with learned word vectors.

The three shared the 2018 Turing Award for deep learning. Their work — backpropagation, convolutional networks, neural language models — is the bridge between the perceptron’s promise and the transformer’s scale. The line from Rosenblatt to GPT bends through two winters, and it stays continuous: the same bet on learning from data, carried through both.

The aha

The return was scale plus a better learning method.

Backpropagation, data, GPUs, and architectural luck compounded. The winters belonged to the story too.

09 The LLM line

Attention, then pretraining, then scale.

The 2017 paper “Attention Is All You Need” was written to improve machine translation. Vaswani and his coauthors replaced the step-by-step recurrence of earlier sequence models with self-attention: in one layer, every token can look at every other token, and the computation for all positions runs in parallel. The path between two distant tokens becomes a single hop. That helps long-range dependencies, and it fills a GPU far better than a loop over the sequence.

The mechanism is a learned routing operation. Each token is projected into a query, a key, and a value. The query of one token is compared with the keys of the others; the scores are scaled, passed through a softmax, and used as weights to mix the values. A causal mask in a decoder hides future tokens, so the model cannot see the answer it is supposed to predict. Several heads run in parallel, each in its own subspace, and their outputs are combined.

Attention is one half of the block. Each sublayer is wrapped in a residual connection and a normalization step: the branch computes an update, and the update is added back to a shared running vector. The feed-forward network then transforms each position on its own, expanding the vector, applying a nonlinearity, and contracting it again. Attention moves information between positions; the feed-forward network computes with it.

Two branches grew from the same architecture. BERT (Devlin et al., 2018) kept the encoder, removed the causal mask, and trained with masked language modeling: hide a random selection of tokens and predict them from both sides. That gives deeply bidirectional representations. After pretraining on a large corpus, BERT could be fine-tuned with a small labelled dataset and set new records on many language tasks. It made the recipe pretrain, then fine-tune the default.

GPT took the other branch: a decoder-only transformer trained autoregressively on the next token. GPT-2 (2019) showed that a large model could perform some tasks zero-shot, from a prompt alone. GPT-3 (Brown et al., 2020), with 175 billion parameters, showed that scale could produce a flexible few-shot learner. The same block, two contracts with the data: BERT sees both sides and fills blanks; GPT sees only the past and predicts the next token.

The recipe then became an engineering programme. ULMFiT supplied the three-step template: pretrain on general text, fine-tune on the target domain, fine-tune on the task. RoBERTa showed that BERT had been undertrained, and that more data with dynamic masking improved it. ELECTRA changed the objective from filling blanks to detecting replaced tokens, which gives a training signal at every position. T5 turned every task into text-to-text.

Chinchilla (Hoffmann et al., 2022) showed that, for a fixed compute budget, many models were too large for the amount of data they saw; a smaller model trained on more tokens can do better. The rough rule of thumb from that work is about twenty training tokens per parameter, though later models often go further. LLaMA applied that lesson and released models that could run on ordinary hardware, which helped the open-weight ecosystem grow.

The final layer is post-training. A raw next-token predictor is not yet a helpful assistant. RLHF — reinforcement learning from human feedback — trains a reward model on human preferences and then optimizes the language model against it. InstructGPT (Ouyang et al., 2022) described the recipe, and ChatGPT turned it into a public interface. The model still predicts tokens; the difference is that the distribution has been shaped toward answers people find useful, honest, and harmless.

Why the same block can be an encoder or a decoder

The mask and the training objective decide the contract. BERT’s encoder reads the whole sentence and fills masked gaps. GPT’s decoder reads only the past and predicts the next token. An encoder–decoder model such as the original transformer reads the source and generates the target. The block is shared; the information flow is different.

The aha

The transformer made context parallel.

Recurrence forced the model to carry the past through a chain. Self-attention lets every token ask every other token what it needs — and that one change made both long-range context and large-scale training practical.

There is a second, smaller alphabet underneath all of this: the tokenizer. A model does not read words or letters; it reads a fixed table of fragments, usually built with byte-pair encoding. Common words become one token; rare words split into pieces. The table is fixed before training, so it quietly decides what is cheap, what is expensive, and what the model can represent at all. The tokenizer lab shows the mechanics.

10 What the history teaches

A collective, uneven climb.

The standard story is a parade of famous names and sudden breakthroughs. The actual history is slower and more collective. Counting devices appear independently in southern Africa, central Europe, and central Africa. The first neural network paper lands in a biophysics journal. The perceptron is announced by the U.S. Navy, criticised, and survives in the margins until the hardware and the data catch up.

There were winters: periods when the promises outran the results and the money left. There were also quiet accumulations — the stored-program idea, the transistor, the GPU, the dataset, the optimizer — without which the same algorithm would be a curiosity. The transformer condensed a very long fuse.

For the material and human history the standard parade leaves out — the labour, the mines, the women who wrote the first programs, the technicians who wired the machines — the wider history continues beyond this page. That is the other half of this story.
