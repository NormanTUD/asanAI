Language is a very old technology.

Before text could become training data, humans had to build the machinery for it: a vocal tract, symbols, writing, and a way to measure information. This is the long fuse behind every token.

01 The idea

Speech came first.

Every language model is downstream of a biological accident: the human vocal tract can make a much wider range of sounds than those of other primates. That made spoken language possible, and spoken language made culture cumulative.

But speech is evanescent. Writing turned it into something that could survive the speaker, travel across distance, and be re-read. Oral tradition already used rhythm and story as memory technology; writing made memory external and inspectable.

02 The long fuse

From grammar to information.

Grammar was studied long before machines. Pāṇini's Sanskrit grammar described language as a system of rules more than two thousand years ago. Later, printing, dictionaries, and standardised spelling made text abundant and comparable.

In the twentieth century, Shannon separated the message from its meaning and measured information in bits. That move made language computable. A model does not need to understand a sentence to predict the next token; it needs a representation and a lot of text.

The last step is geometric: tokens become vectors, and meaning becomes a pattern of distances and directions. The model inherits the whole stack — body, symbol, writing, information — and compresses it into weights.

03 What the model inherits

From text to vectors.

Before a model can learn anything, text has to become a sequence of tokens. A tokenizer cuts the string into pieces from a fixed vocabulary: whole words, word fragments, punctuation, and bytes. The token ID is the only thing the model sees; the original characters are gone.

Each token ID is looked up in an embedding table and becomes a vector. The distributional idea is old: words that appear in similar contexts tend to have similar meanings, so their vectors end up close together. A direction in that space can carry a relationship, such as the direction from 'man' to 'woman'.

That is the inheritance. Writing made text durable, Shannon made it measurable, and embeddings made it geometric. The model does not need a theory of language to learn the geometry; it needs enough text for the geometry to become useful.

The History of Writing

#### Cave Paintings and Symbolic Art The earliest forms of “proto-writing” began with symbolic art. These were the first instances of exogrammatic storage, information kept outside the human brain.

What makes these hand stencils significant in the trajectory toward artificial intelligence is not their aesthetic beauty but their cognitive implication: each handprint is an act of self-referential symbolic displacement. The artist uses their own body as a template to produce a persistent, external representation of themselves, a mark that says “I was here” long after the hand is withdrawn. This is the same cognitive operation that underlies all subsequent abstraction: the realization that a sign can stand for a thing, and that the sign persists independently of the thing it represents. From hand stencils to tally marks, from tally marks to numerals, from numerals to variables, from variables to embeddings, the trajectory is unbroken. The bone pipe that sprayed ochre onto a Patagonian cliff face 9,000 years ago performed, in miniature, the same operation as a modern tokenizer: transforming a continuous, embodied reality into a discrete, storable, transmissible representation.

After the Flood: Information Is Not Meaning

That distinction is the cleanest way to state what a language model does and does not do. An LLM is a machine for estimating the probability of the next token from a vast corpus; it is exquisitely sensitive to the statistical structure of the flood, and it has no independent, non-statistical purchase on whether a sentence is true, important, or worth saying. Shannon deliberately excluded meaning from his theory, and the exclusion is still built into the objective: cross-entropy rewards a good guess about what comes next, not a good answer about what is the case.

The history in this chapter therefore ends with a question rather than a triumph. Writing externalized memory; printing and the telegraph externalized and accelerated its transmission; Shannon made its quantity measurable; the computer made it machine-processable; and the LLM has now made a statistical model of the whole archive. What remains outside the model is the part the archive was always for: the act of a mind that means something by it. Whether that act can itself be captured by information, or whether it is the one thing the flood cannot carry, is the question the rest of this course takes up.

Language as a Window onto the World

If language is a system of abstract symbols, what does it symbolize? A tempting answer is that every tongue is simply a different set of labels for one shared inventory of things, a single universal list of concepts that each culture happens to pronounce differently. The evidence points somewhere stranger. Language is a window onto the world, and the window is cut differently in each culture. The world as a whole offers an almost inexhaustible number of aspects one could attend to; a language is a historically grown selection, a way of carving that continuum into named pieces, and its speakers learn to look through that particular frame.

When one language names something another does not, the difference is not merely a missing label. A word is a handle: it lets speakers pick out, hold steady, and pass around a particular aspect of experience. A language that has a word for a distinction hands its speakers a tool the other language’s speakers lack, just as a language can fuse into one word what another must laboriously spell out. To learn another language is not to relabel a single world but to acquire a second set of handles on it, and so to see a few more of the aspects it was always possible to see.

The Cognitive Ignition: From Indexical Signaling to Symbolic Displacement

The most profound leap in human evolution was the transition from indexical signaling to symbolic displacement. Early primate communication was largely tied to immediate stimuli, a specific call for a specific predator, right here, right now. True language emerged when our ancestors began using sounds to refer to things absent in time or space. This required a massive expansion in working memory and the cognitive ability to maintain “mental representations.”
