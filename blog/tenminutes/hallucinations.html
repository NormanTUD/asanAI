Plausible is not the same as true.

A language model is a pattern completion engine, not a database. That is the source of its fluency and the source of its most dangerous failure.

01 The idea

It completes patterns.

The model does not look up facts in a table. It has learned, from a very large amount of text, which continuations usually follow which contexts. Ask for a medical study and it knows what a study title looks like; it can produce a convincing one that does not exist.

This is not lying in the human sense. The model has no intention to deceive and no internal fact-checker. It is doing the only thing it was trained to do: assigning probability to the next token.

02 Why it sounds confident

Confidence is not evidence.

Confidence is a property of the text, not of the model's knowledge. A fluent answer with a plausible citation can be wrong in every detail. Temperature makes this worse: a higher temperature flattens the distribution and can push the model toward less likely, more inventive continuations.

Use the model as a draft, a tutor, or a search aid — not as an oracle. Verify names, numbers, quotes, and citations. Give it documents with retrieval, ask it to show its sources, and keep a human in the loop for anything that matters.

03 A practical checklist

How to use a plausible machine.

Treat the output as a draft, not an answer. Verify names, dates, numbers, quotations, and citations against a source you trust. If the model gives you a study, a law, or a URL, check that it exists. A fluent paragraph is not evidence of a real source.

Use retrieval and tools when the answer depends on documents. Ask the model to quote the passage it is relying on, and keep the retrieved text visible. For anything that matters, separate generation from verification: let the model draft, then check the draft with a different process or a different model.

Temperature and decoding change how adventurous the model is, not whether it is right. Low temperature makes the answer more predictable; high temperature makes it more inventive and more likely to drift. Neither setting installs a fact-checker. The workflow has to do that.

Background

The Problem: Why AI Lies to You

One of the biggest dangers of modern AI (Large Language Models) is that they can sound incredibly confident while saying something completely untrue. We call this a Hallucination.

To understand why this happens, we have to stop thinking of AI as a “Database of Knowledge” and start thinking of it as a Pattern Completion Engine. Deliberately over-driving that engine — tuning the input instead of the weights — is what the Inceptionism dreams do: the network projects its learned templates onto noise until it sees towers in rocks and birds in clouds, engineered pareidolia.

Background

Problems with AI

#### The Training Data Cutoff and Zeitgeist Bias A primary driver of hallucinations is the static nature of an AI's knowledge. Because models are trained up to a specific point in time, they often “invent” information about events occurring after that date, attempting to force new facts into existing, outdated patterns. This is compounded by popularity bias: the AI tends to provide the statistically most likely answer even if it is incorrect. If training data contains a myth more frequently than the historical truth, the AI will reproduce it as fact because the linguistic pattern is simply stronger.

#### The Black Box of Interpretability A deep-seated challenge remains the lack of transparency in neural networks. Even at a low Temperature, it is often impossible to pinpoint exactly why a specific neuron “fired” to trigger a hallucination. The field of Mechanistic Interpretability is currently working to decode these internal decision paths. The goal is to prevent hallucinations at the source, within the model's weights, rather than simply filtering the output after the fact.

More background

The Mechanism: Next-Token Prediction

At its core, an LLM is a giant mathematical function that calculates probabilities. It looks at the text you have written so far (the Context) and calculates a probability score for every single word in its vocabulary to see which one should come next.
