Meaning is
geometry.

An embedding turns a token into a point in a high-dimensional space. Similar meanings land near each other; relationships become directions. Move the points, follow the arrows, and see why king − man + woman can land on queen — and where the metaphor breaks.

01 One dimension

A word becomes a number.

Start with a single axis: temperature. Give Freezing the coordinate −30, Cold 5, Warm 35, and Boiling 100. In this one-dimensional world, “Cold” is mathematically close to “Freezing” and far from “Boiling”. You can even do approximate arithmetic: Boiling − Hot ≈ Warm.

The coordinates are hand-assigned here. In a real model nobody writes them down: the model discovers a position for every token from the company it keeps in billions of sentences. One axis is already a model of similarity; language needs many.

What the coordinate means

There is no “temperature neuron” in a real model. A dimension is a latent feature — a direction the optimization found useful, not a label a human chose. The 1-D line is a teaching device.

02 The semantic map

Relationships are reusable arrows.

In two or three dimensions we can draw the space. Each word is a point. The arrow from man to woman is the same arrow as king to queen, and uncle to aunt: gender is one direction you can pick up and carry to another word. Nobody typed that rule in. It fell out of the statistics of the text.

Click a word to see its nearest neighbours. Or choose an analogy and watch the arithmetic land near a word. The map is a toy projection of a much higher-dimensional space; the directions are real in the toy, and the principle is what matters.

03 The metric

Cosine similarity: direction is meaning.

How close are two meanings? Euclidean distance measures the straight-line gap. Cosine similarity measures the angle between the vectors: 1 means they point the same way, 0 means they are unrelated, −1 means opposite. Modern embeddings usually prefer cosine because it is scale-invariant: a long vector and a short vector pointing the same way count as identical.

Magnitude often varies for reasons that have nothing to do with meaning — frequent tokens accumulate more gradient updates and can grow longer. Cosine is blind to that artefact. It is not a perfect measure of meaning, but it asks a cleaner question: do these tokens point in the same direction?

TakeawayCosine asks whether two vectors point the same way. Magnitude can change without changing the angle — which is why direction is the better default.
In high dimensions, almost all random vectors are nearly orthogonal. That is one reason a “nearest neighbour” can feel surprising: the space is vast, and absolute distance is less informative than direction.
04 What is really there

The map is useful. Do not mistake it for the territory.

It is tempting to label axes as “Gender”, “Power”, or “Temperature”. In real models, dimensions are latent features: patterns discovered through co-occurrence, most of which have no name in any human language. We can find directions that correlate with human concepts, but the model does not “understand” a king; it places the token in a set of geometric relationships.

The classic embedding table is also only the first layer. Word2vec, GloVe, and fastText gave every word one static vector. ELMo and BERT made the vector contextual: “bank” in “river bank” and “bank account” gets different representations. In a transformer, the input embedding is immediately rewritten by attention and the residual stream.

Common misreading “Each dimension has a human name.” Most do not. A direction can correlate with gender or royalty without being a clean axis.
Better frame Meaning is a region, not a coordinate. A concept is a neighbourhood, a direction, or a subspace — often distributed across many dimensions.
Common misreading “Cosine similarity proves understanding.” It proves geometric alignment. The interpretation of that alignment is a separate, contested question.
Better frame “Direction is meaning, magnitude is often noise” works as a default. Anisotropy and norm effects complicate it.

Two more ideas are worth carrying: polysemanticity and superposition — a single neuron often responds to several unrelated features because the model compresses more concepts than it has dimensions; and the manifold hypothesis — real data lies on a lower-dimensional surface inside the huge ambient space, which is why high-dimensional models can generalize instead of merely memorizing.

The longer treatment pushes further into Voronoi cells (the territory of each token), persistent homology (holes in the semantic manifold), hyperbolic embeddings (hierarchies), and the geometry of negation. The ten-minute version keeps one sentence: the model computes with positions and directions, and meaning is what those positions do when we read them.
