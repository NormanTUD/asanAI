Fit the pattern, not the noise.

A model can be too simple to see the rule or so flexible that it memorizes every accident. Generalization lives between those two failures.

01 The two failures

Too simple or too flexible.

Underfitting is a model that is too simple. It misses the real pattern and performs badly on both training and new data. Overfitting is the opposite: the model is flexible enough to memorize the training examples, including their noise, and then fails when the noise changes.

The classic demonstration is a polynomial fit. A straight line underfits a sine wave; a very high-degree polynomial can pass through every training point and still oscillate wildly between them.

02 How to tell the difference

Test on what it has not seen.

Measure performance on data the model did not train on. If training improves while validation gets worse, you are overfitting. If both stay bad, you are underfitting.

The fix is not always 'more data' or 'a bigger model'. It can be a simpler model, regularization, early stopping, or a training set that better represents the world. The goal is not to fit the past; it is to generalize to the future.

03 The extrapolation trap

Outside the data.

Interpolation is the easy case: the test point lies between examples the model has seen. Extrapolation is the hard case: the test point lies outside the range of the training data. A model can look excellent inside its range and fail outside it, even when the training error is low.

The bias-variance trade-off explains part of this. A simple model has high bias: it misses the pattern. A flexible model has high variance: it follows the noise. Regularisation, early stopping, more data, and a simpler model all try to move the balance toward generalisation.

The practical test is always held-out data, ideally from a distribution that resembles the real world. A benchmark is a proxy; a low test error is not a guarantee that the model will behave well on the next surprise.

Practical notes

More signal, less noise.

More data helps if it is representative. Regularisation, such as weight decay or dropout, discourages the model from relying on brittle noise. Early stopping uses validation performance to decide when to stop training.

A simpler model can generalise better; a bigger model can overfit. The bias-variance trade-off is not a law that tells you which one to choose; it is a way to reason about the failure.

The best test is a held-out set that resembles the real world, plus a stress test on the cases you care about. A perfect training score is not the goal.

The fine print

Where it still breaks.

A low test error on one dataset is not a guarantee of generalisation. If the real world differs from the test set, the model can fail in ways the benchmark never sees. Distribution shift, rare cases, and adversarial inputs are the hard part.

The best practice is to test on the cases you care about, not only on a random split. A model that is excellent on average can still be dangerous on the edge. The gap between training and deployment is the gap that matters.
