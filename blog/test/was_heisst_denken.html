The Central Question

Heidegger's lecture series circles around one deceptively simple question — "Was heißt Denken?" (What is called thinking?) — and reveals it to be the most profound question of our age.

"Das Bedenklichste in unserer bedenklichen Zeit ist, daß wir noch nicht denken."

— The most thought-provoking thing in our thought-provoking time is that we are still not thinking.
01
The Provocation
Heidegger claims that despite all our science, philosophy, and intellectual activity, we have "still not" begun to truly think.
This is not pessimism. The "noch nicht" (not yet) contains a hidden promise — we are already on the way toward thinking. The withdrawal of what calls us to think is itself the most thought-provoking event. What withdraws from us draws us along with it — we are like migratory birds pulled toward what recedes.
↓ Read more
02
Science Does Not Think
One of Heidegger's most provocative claims: "Die Wissenschaft denkt nicht" — science does not think. This is not an insult but a structural observation.
Sciences operate within pre-given domains (history, nature, language) but can never question the essence of those domains. A historian cannot historically determine what history is. A mathematician cannot mathematically show what the mathematical is. The essence of their fields is the matter of thinking — and there is an unbridgeable gap between thinking and the sciences. No bridge, only a leap.
↓ Read more
03
Thinking as Hand-Work
Thinking is the simplest and therefore hardest "Handwerk" (handicraft) of the human being.
The hand is not merely a grasping organ — apes have those. The hand reaches, receives, carries, draws, folds in prayer. Every gesture of the hand passes through language. Thinking is itself a kind of craft that requires the whole being, not just the intellect. Like a cabinetmaker who must correspond to the hidden forms sleeping in the wood, the thinker must correspond to what gives itself to be thought.
↓ Read more
04
Two Lecture Series
Part I (Winter 1951–52) engages Nietzsche as the last metaphysician. Part II (Summer 1952) returns to Parmenides and the dawn of Western thought.
The two parts mirror each other: the end and the beginning of Western philosophy. Nietzsche's cry "Die Wüste wächst" (The desert grows) marks the culmination of metaphysics. Parmenides' saying "χρή τό λέγειν τε νοεῖν τ' ἐόν ἔμμεναι" marks its inception. Between them stretches the entire history of what we call "thinking" — a history Heidegger wants us to re-enter, not as historians, but as thinkers.

The Fourfold Question

In Part II, Heidegger reveals that "Was heißt Denken?" is not one question but four — and their unity is what matters. Click each to explore.

Question 1
What does the word "thinking" mean?
What is named by the word "Denken"? What do the words Gedanke, Gedächtnis, Dank tell us?
Question 2
What does the tradition understand by thinking?
How has the inherited doctrine — Logic — defined and delimited thinking for 2,500 years?
Question 3
What must we bring to think properly?
What is required of us so that we can accomplish thinking in a way true to its essence?
Question 4 — The Decisive One
What calls us into thinking?
What is it that commands, summons, and entrusts us to thinking? This is the governing question.

Lecture Structure

The two lecture series form a single arc — from the end of metaphysics (Nietzsche) back to its dawn (Parmenides). Click any item to expand.

Part I · Lectures I–III
The Provocation: We Are Not Yet Thinking
Heidegger introduces the central claim and its paradoxes. What withdraws from us is what most concerns us.

The opening lectures establish the key paradox: what most gives us to think is that we are not yet thinking. This is not a moral failing but a structural condition — what calls us to think has itself withdrawn. Yet this withdrawal is not nothing; it draws us along. Heidegger invokes Hölderlin's line "Ein Zeichen sind wir, deutungslos" (We are a sign, without interpretation) from the hymn Mnemosyne — the goddess of Memory, mother of the Muses.

He also introduces the gap between thinking and science, the nature of teaching and learning, and thinking as "Hand-Werk" — the simplest and hardest craft.

Part I · Lectures IV–VI
Vorstellen: The Reign of Representation
What is "representing" (Vorstellen)? How has it shaped all previous thinking? The example of the blossoming tree.

Heidegger asks: when we stand before a blossoming tree, where does the "representing" happen? In our heads? In consciousness? Or do we and the tree stand before each other in a mutual presenting? He argues we must leap out of the scientific framework — which reduces the tree to electrical charges — and land on the ground where we actually live and die.

The entire history of Western thought may be the history of Vorstellen (representation) — a mode of thinking that places everything before itself as an object. This is the deep structure Heidegger wants to make visible.

Part I · Lectures VI–X
Nietzsche: The Desert Grows
Nietzsche as the last thinker of the West. The Übermensch, the spirit of revenge, and the eternal return of the same.

Heidegger reads Nietzsche not as a cultural critic but as a metaphysician. "Die Wüste wächst: weh dem, der Wüsten birgt!" — The desert grows: woe to him who harbors deserts! The "last man" blinks — his mode of representation reduces everything to surfaces. The Übermensch is not a super-powered human but one who crosses over from the old human essence.

The bridge to this crossing is "Erlösung von der Rache" — redemption from the spirit of revenge. Revenge is the will's ill-will against time and its "it was." The eternal return of the same is Nietzsche's attempt to free the will from this revenge — to will the return of everything, including the past. This is the summit of Western metaphysics.

Part II · Lectures I–IV
The Fourfold Question & the Word "Denken"
The four ways of asking "What is called thinking?" The old word "Gedanc" — heart, memory, thanks.

Part II begins fresh. Heidegger unfolds the fourfold question and shows that the fourth — "What calls us to think?" — is the governing one. He then explores the German word "Denken" through its old form "der Gedanc," which means the gathered heart, the innermost of the human being. From this root spring Gedächtnis (memory as devoted attention), Dank (thanks), and Andenken (commemorative thinking).

The deepest thanks would be: simply to think what most gives itself to be thought. Thinking is thanking.

Part II · Lectures V–VIII
Logic, Λόγος, and the Saying of Parmenides
Why is the doctrine of thinking called "Logic"? What does λέγειν really mean? Translating Parmenides' saying.

Heidegger traces "Logic" back to λόγος and λέγειν. But λέγειν originally means "to lay" and "to let lie before" — not primarily "to say." Saying, for the Greeks, is a mode of letting-lie-before, of letting what is present appear. Similarly, νοεῖν is not "thinking" in our sense but "taking-into-regard" — a caring attentiveness.

The Parmenides fragment χρή τό λέγειν τε νοεῖν τ' ἐόν ἔμμεναι is painstakingly translated not as "It is necessary to say and think that beings are" but as: "It is needful: the letting-lie-before-us so (the) taking-to-heart also: beings in being."

Part II · Lectures IX–XI
Ἐόν: The Twofold of Being and Beings
The word ἐόν as the "participle of all participles." The Zwiefalt (twofold) that grounds all Western metaphysics.

The word ἐόν (being/a being) is grammatically a participle — it participates in both nominal and verbal meaning. "A being" and "being" (in the sense of presencing). This duality — the Zwiefalt — is the unthought ground of all metaphysics. Every philosophical question moves within it, but it has never itself been questioned.

Heidegger translates ἐόν as "Anwesendes anwesend" — the presencing of what is present. From this twofold, the entire history of Western thought unfolds — from Parmenides through Plato's χωρισμός to Kant's conditions of experience to Hegel's absolute logic.

Nietzsche & The Growing Desert

Part I is dominated by Heidegger's reading of Nietzsche as the last metaphysician. Explore the key concepts.

"Die Wüste wächst: weh dem, der Wüsten birgt!"

— The desert grows: woe to him who harbors deserts! (Nietzsche, Also sprach Zarathustra IV)
I
The Last Man (Der letzte Mensch)
The human who can no longer surpass himself. He "blinks" — his mode of representation reduces everything to manageable surfaces.
"We have invented happiness," say the last men, and blink. The last man lives longest — his reign is not an ending but a strange durability. His "blinking" is not mere eye-twitching but the fundamental mode of modern representation: presenting everything as surface, as what can be agreed upon without further inquiry. This is the metaphysical ground of the modern age.

II
The Übermensch
Not a super-powered human, but one who crosses over from the old essence. "Poorer, simpler, tenderer and harder, quieter and more sacrificing."
Heidegger insists the Übermensch is not a titan or a dictator. He is the one who first brings the essence of the previous human to its truth and takes it over. He is "Caesar with the soul of Christ." The Übermensch never appears in the noisy processions of supposed power-holders. His appearance remains inaccessible to reporters and the public.
↓ Read more
III
The Spirit of Revenge (Geist der Rache)
"The spirit of revenge: my friends, that has been humanity's best thinking until now." — Zarathustra
Revenge is the will's ill-will against time and its "it was." The will cannot will backwards — the past stands as an immovable stone. This generates resentment at the core of all willing. Revenge names itself "punishment" and cloaks its hostility in the appearance of justice. For Nietzsche, all previous representation is secretly governed by this spirit.
↓ Read more
IV
Eternal Return of the Same
The bridge from revenge to freedom. The will that eternally wills the return of everything — including the "it was."
If the will can will the eternal return of everything, then nothing is merely past. The "it was" returns. The will is freed from its ill-will against time. This is the summit of Western metaphysics — "the extreme approximation of a world of becoming to a world of being." Yet this summit remains shrouded in clouds, even for Nietzsche himself. The thought remains "the heaviest thought."

The word ἐόν names both at once — it is the "participle of all participles." Every philosophical question moves within this twofold, but the twofold itself has never been questioned. It fell into oblivion at the very beginning of Western thought.
Plato's χωρισμός
Plato interprets the twofold as a separation of places — beings and Being are differently located. The Ideas dwell apart from sensible things.
This interpretation of the twofold as "participation" (μέθεξις) becomes the template for all subsequent metaphysics. But the twofold itself — the fact that there IS a difference between beings and Being — is never questioned. It is simply used.
↓ Read more
Kant's Transcendental Turn
Kant's supreme principle — "the conditions of possibility of experience are at the same time conditions of possibility of objects of experience" — is a variation on Parmenides.
The "zugleich" (at the same time) is Kant's interpretation of Parmenides' τό αὐτό (the Same). Thinking and Being belong together. But for Kant, Being appears as the objectivity of objects, and thinking as the representing of the transcendental subject.
↓ Read more
Hegel's Absolute
"That Being is Thinking" — Hegel transposes Kant's principle into the Absolute. Logic becomes the self-organization of Spirit.
For Hegel, "Logic" reaches its highest dignity: it names the element in which the Absolute Spirit spreads out its moments. This is the grandest variation on Parmenides' theme — but it still moves within the unquestioned twofold.
↓ Read more
The Unthought
The twofold itself — the "between" of beings and Being — has never been thought. It fell into forgetting at the very dawn of philosophy.
This forgetting is not a failure. The beginning conceals itself in the commencement. The twofold is the most used and therefore most overlooked structure in all thinking. To question it is to step outside the entire tradition of metaphysics — which is precisely what Heidegger's question "Was heißt Denken?" attempts.
