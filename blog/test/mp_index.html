The wager

If a neuron either fires or does not fire, then thought has a syntax.

In 1943, a psychiatrist and a teenage logician asked what happens if you treat the all-or-none nerve impulse as a proposition. The answer became the founding paper of neural computation: every net has a logic, every logic has a net, and nets with circles can remember a world that has already passed.

The fifteen plates below follow that idea outward — into probability, perception, value, ethics, and the stubborn question of why the mind is in the head. Each plate is built from the paper itself: its arguments, its formulas, its figures, its voice.

How to read: every plate has a hero canvas, a short thesis, a working mechanism you can move, the paper's own best sentences, and facsimile pages you can open. Nothing is a metaphor unless the paper says so.

Sources & method

No paraphrase without a page.

Every plate is keyed to an actual PDF: the Vordenker editions of the 1943 calculus, the 1945 heterarchy, the 1951 Hixon lecture, the 1951 Thalès lecture, the 1956 ethical-robot essay, and the 1960 Korzybski lecture; the original Bulletin of Mathematical Biophysics and Biometrics scans for the 1943 statistical note, the 1947 universals paper, and the 1948 statistical organization; the 1955 Scientific Monthly for Mysterium Iniquitatis; the 1959 IEEE reprint of the frog's-eye paper; and the 1974 ASC Forum memoir.

The three literary papers are keyed to the Chicago Literary Club editions: the 1945 tribute to Robinson (public-domain scan at HathiTrust), the 1959 poems (search-only at HathiTrust; text from Embodiments of Mind), and the 1953 polemic (Vordenker bilingual edition).

Quotes are short and verbatim. Formulas are rendered from the papers' own notation, with a modern reading added where the typography of 1943 needs translation. Facsimiles are page images from the PDFs, not redrawn illustrations.
