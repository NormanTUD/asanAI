The Metaphysics of an Experimental Epistemologist

Heinz von Foerster's essay on Warren McCulloch is not a summary but a portrait of a method: point, decide, take responsibility. Its centre is the distinction between questions that are already decided by their framework and questions that are in principle undecidable — and therefore ours to decide.

The thesis

We become metaphysicians whenever we decide upon in principle undecidable questions.

Von Foerster begins with Seymour Papert's favourite teaching aid: “Don't bite my finger, look where I am pointing.” McCulloch, he says, is pointing at pointing. The essay follows that gesture through causality, inclusion, the pre-Socratics, Günther's non-Aristotelian logic, Don Juan, and Wittgenstein.

The decisive move is a distinction. A decidable question is already decided by the framework in which it is asked; it may take eight years, but logic will return a definite yes or no. An undecidable question is not like that. We are free to decide it, and then we have to take responsibility for the decision. That freedom is the metaphysical act.

The two answers: “Am I apart from the Universe?” or “Am I part of the Universe?” Both are decisions on an undecidable question. McCulloch chooses to include himself in the world he constructs.

Laboratory · the undecidable stance

Peephole or participation?

Move the dial. At one end, the knower looks through a peephole at a universe that is already finished. At the other, the knower is inside the universe, and every act changes both the world and the actor. Neither end is forced by the evidence. The dial is a decision.

Two undecidables, one dial

The wall, the eye, the actor, and the liar's loop. The loop is the ultimate logical perpetual motion machine: from false it generates true, from true false, and so on.

The distinction

Why the undecidable is where freedom lives.

“Only those questions that are in principle undecidable we can decide.” The sentence sounds paradoxical until one sees the point: a decidable question is already constrained by its framework. The answer is not free; it is forced. An undecidable question leaves the choice open, and with the choice comes responsibility.

Von Foerster's examples run from the squaring of the circle to the origin of the universe, from “what is consciousness?” to “am I apart from the universe?”. The list is not a list of mysteries to be solved. It is a list of places where the knower must take a stand.

The compulsion of logic. “Ultimately — thanks to the inescapable force of logic — we shall be rewarded with a definite ‘Yes’ or a definite ‘No.’”

The freedom of the act. “We are under no compulsion … which way to decide: we are free to decide in this or that way, and then we have to take the responsibility for our decision.”

The perpetual motion machine. The liar's paradox is not a bug in logic but a machine for generating the next truth-value forever.

The coda's place

The method behind the plates.

This essay is the best gloss on the whole codex because it names the intellectual virtue the plates try to practise: do not pretend that an undecidable question has been settled by evidence; decide it, say that you have decided it, and take responsibility. McCulloch's answer — include yourself in the world you describe — is one such decision.

It also explains why the codex ends with a memoir rather than a theorem. The sources are not decoration. They are the framework within which the questions became askable.
