The emergence mirage.

Large language models seem to “suddenly” learn arithmetic, or suddenly fail a benchmark. Often the jump is not in the model. It is in the metric we chose to look at.

02 · It is not just language

The same trick manufactures emergence in vision

The authors induced “never-before-seen” emergent abilities in vision models by choosing a thresholded or all-or-nothing metric. The networks improve smoothly; the metric adds the cliff.

Autoencoders on CIFAR-100

Mean squared reconstruction error falls smoothly as the bottleneck grows. Define “reconstruction ability” as the fraction of images with error below a threshold, and a sharp jump appears.

smooth quality → thresholded fraction

Omniglot classifiers

Per-image classification accuracy rises smoothly with scale. Require the model to classify all five images in a sequence correctly, and the fifth power turns the slope into an apparent cliff.

per-image accuracy → subset accuracy

Why this matters: language models were thought special partly because vision models did not show such abrupt transitions. The paper shows that the transition is a property of the chosen measurement, not of language.

03 · The careful version

What the paper does — and does not — claim

It does not say: “large models never acquire new abilities.” It says that the specific published evidence for sharp, unpredictable emergence is compatible with a much simpler explanation: a smooth underlying improvement measured with a nonlinear or discontinuous yardstick, often on too little test data to resolve small-model performance.

The practical rule: a task and a metric are separate choices. If you use accuracy or exact match, increase the test set until small models are measurable. Report a linear or continuous metric alongside. And remember that BIG-Bench alone offers roughly a million task–metric–model-family triplets, so some apparent cliffs can be selection effects.

04 · Takeaway

Four sentences to remember

The model is smooth

Per-token loss and per-token correctness improve gradually. That is what the scaling laws describe.

The metric can be sharp

“All tokens correct” raises a smooth number to a high power. “Highest-probability option” is a step function. Both turn gradual progress into a cliff.

Resolution matters

With a small test set, a model at 0.3% accuracy looks like a model at 0%. More data reveals the smooth improvement underneath.

Measure twice

Report a continuous metric and a nonlinear one. If the “emergent” ability survives both, it is worth taking seriously.
