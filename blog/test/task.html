Boundary maps, one picture at a time

Everything below is built from a single idea: a shape is glued together from triangles-of-various-dimensions called simplices, and the boundary map ∂ records what the edge/rim/skin of each piece is. Read straight down. The drawings appear by themselves; one of them animates.

Step 0 — What is a simplex? (the building blocks)

Intuition

A simplex is the simplest solid shape you can make from a given number of corners: 0 corners → a point, 2 → a line segment, 3 → a filled triangle, 4 → a solid tetrahedron. An n-simplex just means "the simplex with n+1 corners", so it lives in n dimensions.

Formalism

An n-simplex is written [v0,v1,…,vn] where the vi are its corners, called vertices. It is the filled-in region spanned by those corners.

Example
[v0] = point  ·  [v0,v1] = edge  ·  [v0,v1,v2] = triangle  ·  [v0,v1,v2,v3] = tetrahedron

The four smallest simplices. Notice each one is "the previous one, plus one new corner."

Step 1 — "Order the vertices by index." What does that mean, and why?

Intuition

Writing the corners in a fixed order v0,v1,v2,… does one thing: it puts arrows on all the edges — each edge points from the lower-numbered corner to the higher-numbered one. Those arrows are the "orientation". They are the reason minus signs will show up later: an arrow can agree or disagree with your finger's direction of travel, and that agree/disagree is exactly a + or a −.

Formalism

"Ordered by index" means whenever we look at any sub-simplex (any face), we list its vertices in the same increasing-subscript order as in the big simplex. This choice fixes one orientation per simplex; swapping two vertices flips the sign of that simplex.

Example

On the edge [v0,v1] the arrow goes 0→1. On the triangle [v0,v1,v2] the three edge-arrows 0→1, 1→2, 0→2 run counter-clockwise-ish, which is the picture below.

Left: an oriented edge (one arrow). Right: an oriented triangle (arrows induced by 0<1<2).

Step 2 — Head and tail (and why ∂ of an edge is head − tail)

Intuition

Walk along the arrow of an edge. You start at one corner (the tail) and finish at the other (the head). The boundary of a walk is "where I ended minus where I started". Ending counts +, starting counts −. That's the whole formula for a 1-simplex.

Formalism
∂[vi,vj] = vj − vi = (head) − (tail)

The head is the last vertex listed, the tail is the first.

Example
∂[v0,v1] = v1 − v0

The arrow's start is the tail (−), its finish is the head (+).

Step 3 — What is a chain? (a "shopping list" of simplices)

Intuition

A chain is just a weighted collection of simplices of one fixed dimension — like a shopping list "2 copies of edge a, 1 copy of edge b, but subtract edge c". A minus sign means "that edge, but walked backwards". You never have to picture "2.5 triangles"; the numbers are pure bookkeeping, exactly like coordinates of a vector.

Formalism

"Cn = the vector space of formal ℝ-combinations of the n-simplices" means: take the n-simplices as a basis, and let a chain be any finite sum a1σ1 + a2σ2 + … with real numbers ai. "Formal" = we're allowed to add them and scale them like vectors even though the sum has no single-picture meaning.

Example
the 1-chain   2a + b − c

Thickness shows the coefficient; the red edge has coefficient −1 (its arrow is flipped).

Step 4 — The boundary map (the animation you asked for)

Intuition

The boundary of a filled triangle is its three edges — its rim. The formula builds that rim by a little ritual: delete each corner in turn. Deleting a corner from a triangle leaves the edge that was opposite that corner. Do this for all three corners and you get all three edges. The signs +, −, + are put in so the three edges join up head-to-tail into one coherent loop.

Formalism
∂[v0,…,vn] = Σi (−1)i [v0,…, vi ,…,vn]

The hat vi means "delete this vertex." The "each vertex in turn" refers to the vertices of the very simplex whose boundary you are computing — here the triangle's three corners v0, v1, v2. Deleting vertex number i gives the i-th face, weighted by (−1)i.

Example (watch it run)
∂[v0,v1,v2] = +[v1,v2] −[v0,v2] +[v0,v1]

Step 5 — Why the boundary of a boundary is nothing (∂∘∂ = 0)

Intuition

The rim of the triangle is a closed loop of three edges. Now take the boundary of that — i.e. the head/tail endpoints of every edge, with signs. Every corner is the head of one edge (+) and the tail of the next edge (−). Those +1 and −1 land on the same point and cancel. Nothing survives. A rim has no ends.

Formalism
∂∘∂ = 0
Example

At each corner a + from one edge's head meets a − from the next edge's tail → they cancel → the total boundary is 0.

The dictionary (what all this is for)

Once shapes are chains and ∂ is defined, the "holes" are counted by Betti numbers bn = (# n-simplices) − rank ∂n − rank ∂n+1:

b0 = # separate clusters  ·  b1 = # independent loops  ·  b2 = # enclosed hollow voids
