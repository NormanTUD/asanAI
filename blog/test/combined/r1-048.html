From Sparse Samples to a Surrogate You Can Act On

Fusion aus: botorch.html ✕ space_algorithm.html

Two explainers, one machine. One builds a probabilistic model of an expensive function to choose its next measurement; the other builds a smooth field from a handful of token-movements to render the whole space. Both are the same act: turn a few samples into a continuous, kernel-weighted model of an "everywhere" quantity, then act on it.

Kernthese der Fusion

Both papers rest on the same epistemic move. You are given a handful of samples and asked to say something about everywhere else. The honest way to do that is to impose smoothness with a kernel: each sample carries a Gaussian "influence" that fades with distance, and the value at any unobserved point is a kernel-weighted blend of the samples. botorch.html (BoTorch) does exactly this with a Gaussian process — a mean prediction plus an uncertainty band over an expensive scalar function. space_algorithm.html (the Metric Space Explorer) does the same with RBF interpolation — turning a few token-displacements into a continuous velocity field that deforms a grid. The Gaussian kernel in the first and the RBF in the second are literally the same function, exp(−‖x−xᵢ‖²/(2σ²)).

The fused position is therefore: a GP is the probabilistic, uncertainty-aware version of RBF interpolation; the RBF field is the deterministic, noiseless limit of a GP mean. Where they diverge is only the job the surrogate does. BoTorch's surrogate is instrumental — it exists to pick the single next point worth measuring, balancing exploration (high uncertainty) against exploitation (near the current best) under a budget of expensive evaluations. The space-morpher's field is descriptive — it exists to make the entire transformation legible at once (the full deformed grid, all tokens, all layers, with strain coloring). One output is a decision (a coordinate); the other is a picture of the whole.

Put together, the two are the two ends of one spectrum of kernel surrogate modeling: the space-morpher is "a surrogate with an infinite budget whose output is a rendering," and BoTorch is "a surrogate with a tiny budget whose output is the next probe." Unify them and you get a single framework: build a kernel surrogate (learn its smoothness from the data), keep a full posterior over the quantity of interest, and let an acquisition function decide where the next — expensive — observation should go.

Widersprüche zwischen A und B

Honesty note The two papers come from different fields (hyperparameter optimization vs. transformer interpretability) and make no directly conflicting factual claims. The conflicts are at the level of methodological stance — how you build and how you use a continuous model from sparse data. Three specific, locatable tensions.

TensionA — botorch.html saysB — space_algorithm.html says
1. Uncertainty as first-class vs. silent point estimate The GP is a posterior distribution: it gives "a mean prediction and an uncertainty band (how confident it is)," and "where we don't have data, uncertainty is high." The band drives the whole decision. The RBF interpolant is a single deterministic field. "We apply the interpolated velocity field to deform a regular grid," and the strain map is presented as a measured property of space — no band, no variance, no "we don't know here."
2. The model's job: adaptive next-step decision vs. global one-shot description The surrogate exists "to find the optimum in as few tries as possible." The loop is sequential and adaptive: each new observation refines the model and changes the next decision. Output = one coordinate. The field exists to render the whole at once — deformed grid + all token trajectories + all layers + strain, in one static picture. No budget, no sequence, no decision to make.
3. Smoothness learned from data vs. hand-set by the user "The GP hyperparameters (kernel lengthscale, noise variance, etc.) are fit by maximizing the marginal likelihood using L-BFGS-B via fit_gpytorch_mll()." The width is inferred. σ (kernel width) is a literal slider the user drags; "σ controls how far each token's influence reaches." The width is asserted, not justified.

Auflösung

Tension 1 Reconcile: the field is the GP mean — so wrap it in a posterior. The RBF blend and the GP predictive mean are the same expression when the kernel is Gaussian and the noise → 0. So the space-morpher's "surface" is really the mean deformation, and the resolution is to attach a variance to it: large between tokens, small at tokens. The fused object is a probabilistic vector field — a posterior over deformations, not a single surface. The strain map stops being a number and becomes a range (mean strain ± uncertainty). Fused conclusion: treat every interpolated quantity as mean + fog; the field is a posterior.

Tension 2 Reconcile: description is the infinite-budget limit of the decision loop. If the space-morpher's "observations" (token/layer movements) were expensive to obtain, then an EI/UCB acquisition function over the observation budget tells you which token or layer to probe next — exactly where the posterior is most uncertain or most likely to change the deformation. The acquisition function is the bridge between the two. Fused conclusion: one mechanism (kernel surrogate + acquisition) subsumes both; the morpher is BO with an infinite budget and a picture as the output, BoTorch is the morpher with a scalar field, an expensive field, and the next-probe as the output.

Tension 3 Reconcile: replace the σ slider with a marginal-likelihood fit. What B does by hand (a knob) A does automatically (maximizing the log marginal likelihood). The resolution is to infer the width from the data — by MLL or cross-validation — and keep the slider only as a diagnostic override. Fused conclusion: let the data set the kernel width; then the field is not "an assumption" but "the smoothness the data supports."

Overall fused conclusion Build a kernel surrogate with learned width; carry a full posterior (mean + variance) over the quantity of interest (a scalar f in BO, a vector field δ in the morpher); and use an acquisition function to spend each expensive probe where it matters most. The "deformed grid" is the posterior-mean deformation; its uncertainty fog marks where the next observation belongs. Strain — a functional of the deformation field — inherits the same posterior.

Fusionierter Inhalt

The shared object — a kernel surrogate. Both papers start from sparse samples {(xᵢ, yᵢ)} and predict a quantity at an unobserved point x by blending the samples with a distance kernel. The shared kernel is the Gaussian/RBF:

k(x, xᵢ) = exp( − ‖x − xᵢ‖² / (2σ²) )
Same expression in both papers: BoTorch's GP kernel (σ = lengthscale ℓ) and the morpher's RBF weight (σ = "kernel width").

Where the morpher is the GP's mean. The space-morpher's RBF field (from space_algorithm.html) is a normalized weighted blend of the token deltas:

δ(p) = ( Σᵢ wᵢ(p) · δᵢ ) / ( Σᵢ wᵢ(p) ),   wᵢ(p) = exp( − ‖p − pᵢ‖² / (2σ²) )
A smooth, continuous vector field: "drop a stone in water at each token, the ripples (Gaussian bells) overlap, closer tokens weigh more."

BoTorch's GP (from botorch.html) is the same idea promoted to a distribution. With a prior f ~ N(0, k) and noisy observations y, the posterior at a test point is:

μ*(x) = k(x, X)ᵀ (K + σ²ₙ I)⁻¹ y     var*(x) = k(x, x) − k(x, X)ᵀ (K + σ²ₙ I)⁻¹ k(X, x)
As the noise σₙ → 0 with a pure-Gaussian kernel, μ* reduces exactly to the RBF blend above. The morpher's field is the noiseless GP mean; the GP adds the variance the morpher never computes.

The morpher's pipeline (B). A transformer's hidden state flows through the residual stream; each layer adds a delta:

h⁽ℓ⁺¹⁾ = h⁽ℓ⁾ + f_ℓ(h⁽ℓ⁾)    δ⁽ℓ⁾ = h⁽ℓ⁺¹⁾ − h⁽ℓ⁾    h⁽ᴸ⁾ = h⁽⁰⁾ + Σ_ℓ δ⁽ℓ⁾
Deltas stack head-to-tail: the final position is the embedding plus all layer deltas.

These high-D states are projected to 2D by PCA (center → covariance → top-2 eigenvectors), the per-token deltas are turned into a field by RBF interpolation, the field deforms a grid, and strain measures local distortion:

p′ = p + t·amp·δ(p)   (multi-layer: p⁽ℓ⁺¹⁾ = p⁽ℓ⁾ + t·amp·δ⁽ℓ⁾(p⁽ℓ⁾), applied sequentially)
strainᵢⱼ = ‖p′ⱼ − p′ᵢ‖ / ‖pⱼ − pᵢ‖   (>1 expand, <1 contract, =1 isometric)
"If the entire space were made of rubber, how would each layer stretch, compress, and twist it?"

The BO loop (A). BoTorch runs the opposite direction — it chooses samples rather than rendering them. Each iteration: fit the GP (hyperparameters by maximizing the marginal likelihood) → approximate the acquisition function by Monte-Carlo samples using the reparameterization trick ξ = μ + σ·ε (so the acqf is differentiable) → fix those samples once (SAA) so the objective is deterministic → optimize it with multi-start L-BFGS-B from Sobol initializations → evaluate the true (expensive) function there → add to the data. The acquisition scores each candidate by balancing exploration vs. exploitation:

EI(x) = (μ(x) − f_best)·Φ(z) + σ(x)·φ(z),   z = (μ(x) − f_best)/σ(x)
UCB(x) = μ(x) + β·σ(x)     POI(x) = Φ(z)
SAA converges to the true optimizer fast: plain MC is O(1/√N), quasi-MC (Sobol) is O(1/N) — "the QMC approximation only requires 64 samples" to match 4096 plain MC samples.

The unified algorithm. Read both as one procedure over a quantity of interest q(x) that is a scalar f (BO) or a vector δ (morpher):

StepIn botorch.html (decision mode)In space_algorithm.html (description mode)
1. Sparse samplesexpensive function evaluations (budgeted)token deltas at layer boundaries (all present)
2. Kernel surrogateGP posterior (mean + variance)RBF field (mean only)
3. Width σ / ℓfit by marginal likelihoodhand-set slider
4. Actacquisition → next probe (EI/UCB/POI)render full deformation + strain
5. Outputone coordinate (the next decision)the whole map (a picture)

The fusion collapses the table: the deformed grid is the posterior-mean deformation, the uncertainty fog is where to spend the next probe, and the acquisition function is what turns a description into a decision when samples are expensive.
