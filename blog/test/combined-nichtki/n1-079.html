The signal as a shadow: unprojecting it back to space and back to frequency

Fusion aus: takens_embedding_theorem.html ✕ signal_analysis.html

1 Kernthese der Fusion

Both papers open on the same starting point: you are handed a single one-dimensional time series x(t), and you want to know the richer object it came from. A — Takens says that series is the projection of a hidden dynamical system (a weather field, a beating heart, a chaotic attractor) and that you can unproject it back to space: stack time-delayed copies of the same measurement into Φ(t) = [x(t), x(t−τ), …, x(t−(d−1)τ)] and, for a large enough d, the result is a faithful geometric portrait of the original attractor. B — Signal Analysis says the series is a projection of a hidden set of sinusoids and that you can unproject it back to frequency: correlate it with complex exponentials (the DFT) and the answer is the amplitude and phase of every harmonic that built the signal.

The single integrated position: a time series is a one-dimensional shadow of a higher-dimensional object, and both papers are “unprojection” algorithms — Takens lifts the shadow into state space (geometry and dynamics), the DFT lifts it into frequency space (spectral ingredients). The two reconstructions are not rivals; they are two complementary coordinates of the same data. One tells you what shape the state lives on and how it moves; the other tells you which sinusoids, with what strength and phase, compose the projection.

What unifies them is a shared, quantitative backbone: the unprojection succeeds exactly when the reconstruction space is large enough to hold the object’s intrinsic complexity. On the Takens side that is counted in geometric dimensions — d ≥ 2m+1 for an m-dimensional attractor. On the Fourier side it is counted in frequency bins / sample rate — f_s ≥ 2·f_max (Nyquist). Below that bound both theories predict the same failure, in two guises: self-overlap / false neighbors (A) is the geometric twin of aliasing / spectral leakage (B).

2 Widersprüche zwischen A und B

The two papers do not contradict each other in any factual claim — each is correct inside its own domain, and both are about “recovery from a reduced measurement.” But they take mutually incompatible stances on what the signal is assumed to be, and three real tensions follow directly from their own wording.

1. What the signal is: nonlinear geometry

A — Takens

The series is the projection of a deterministic, nonlinear, possibly chaotic dynamical system. The reconstruction is model-free and geometric — it recovers the specific attractor, its strange shape, and invariants like Lyapunov exponents and fractal dimension. A explicitly leans on chaos: “even though the Lorenz system is chaotic (sensitive to initial conditions), Takens’ embedding lets us make short-term predictions by leveraging the geometry of the attractor.”

1. What the signal is: a linear sum of sines

B — Signal Analysis

The series is a linear superposition of sinusoids — stationary, harmonic, built from “simple building blocks.” B’s own premise: “this is the foundation of Fourier analysis: any signal is a sum of sinusoids.” The entire toolkit (Fourier series, DFT, filtering) is linear. Chaos and nonlinearity are never its subject.

2. Inversion target: back to space

A — Takens

The answer is a shape — the invariant set in &RBB;^d and the dynamics on it (trajectory, loops, holes, exponents). The question answered is “what geometry does the state live on, and how does it move?”

2. Inversion target: back to frequency

B — Signal Analysis

The answer is a decomposition — a fixed global basis of sinusoids with amplitude and phase. The question answered is “which frequencies are present, at what strength, and with what phase?” It is the same data, read as a spectrum rather than as a trajectory.

3. Stationarity: inherently aperiodic

A — Takens

A’s canonical signal (a Lorenz projection) is aperiodic and non-stationary; its spectrum is broadband, with no discrete set of clean frequencies to recover.

3. Stationarity: presumes clean spectra

B — Signal Analysis

B’s clean-spectrum / Nyquist analysis is cleanest for stationary signals, and B itself concedes the limit: “Fourier analysis tells you WHAT frequencies are present but not WHEN they occur,” which is exactly why B must bolt on wavelets for non-stationary data.

Net: no fact-level contradiction. The sharpest real tension is B’s premise “any signal is a sum of sinusoids” versus A’s object — a chaotic, aperiodic, non-harmonic projection — where the DFT of A’s signal would be broadband and uninformative, precisely the case where A’s delay embedding is the right tool.

3 Auflösung

1. Complementary coordinates, not rivals. Fourier recovers the linear / stationary / spectral coordinate of the shadow; Takens recovers the nonlinear / geometric / dynamical coordinate. A complete “reconstruction from a projection” toolbox uses both: the spectrum is the right read for stationary, harmonic content (and it hands you phase for free), while the delay embedding is the right read for the nonlinear, aperiodic content. Fused conclusion: read the same x(t) in two bases — the sinusoid basis (B) and the delay basis (A) — and the full picture is the union.

2. Both are “undo a linear projection by a generic lifting map.” Takens’ delay map Φ is linear in the signal values; it is a faithful embedding (an injection whose inverse is continuous on the attractor) generically, and Takens’ theorem gives the threshold: d ≥ 2m+1. The DFT is likewise a linear map from a sampled signal to the frequency domain, and it is invertible (Parseval: no information lost) once you keep all N bins / sample above Nyquist. Fused conclusion: the two “big enough” theorems are the same statement about matching reconstruction dimension to intrinsic complexity.

3. The failure modes are twins. Push the reconstruction below its bound and both collapse the same way: too few delay dimensions → the attractor folds over itself, producing false nearest neighbors (A); too few samples / below Nyquist → high frequencies fold into lower ones, producing aliasing (B). Above the bound both are generically invertible, and you can move freely among the time, space, and frequency views of the one shadow. Wavelets are the meeting point: a wavelet is time-local geometry (A) times frequency-local content (B) — the coordinate system where the two reconstructions meet.

4 Fusionierter Inhalt

Shared lead-motif — the signal as a shadow. In both papers the observed series x(t) is a projection: a lossy one-dimensional image of a higher-dimensional object. Understanding the system = inverting the projection. The only open question is where the object lives — in state space (A) or in frequency space (B) — and the only real constraint is a lower bound on reconstruction dimension.

A — Takens’ delay embedding (unproject to space)

A single scalar measurement, unfolded into a vector of its own time-delayed copies,

Φ(t) = ( x(t), x(t−τ), x(t−2τ), …, x(t−(d−1)τ) )    d ≥ 2m + 1

is, generically, diffeomorphic to the true attractor once the embedding dimension d exceeds 2m+1 (m = attractor dimension). That one fact buys a lot:

  • Topology preserved — loops, holes, connectivity; the reconstructed set has the same shape class as the original.
  • Dynamics preserved — trajectories map to trajectories, so you can read the motion off the reconstruction.
  • Invariants computable — Lyapunov exponents (the rate of divergence, i.e. the degree of chaos) and fractal dimensions can be measured on the shadow.
  • Prediction — nearest-neighbor forecasting (Empirical Dynamic Modeling): find the current state’s neighbors in the embedding, average where they went next, and forecast — even for chaotic systems, over short horizons.

Choosing the parameters is the craft. The delay τ: too small and x(t−τ) ≈ x(t), so the points pile up on the diagonal (no new information); too large and the lags are uncorrelated, so the structure dissolves into a noise cloud; in between the attractor unfolds cleanly. The dimension d: the False Nearest Neighbors method scans d and stops where the percentage of false neighbors drops to ≈ 0. Applications listed by A: cardiology (single-lead ECG), weather/climate, finance, neuroscience (EEG), engineering (vibration), ecology.

B — Fourier / DFT / wavelets (unproject to frequency)

The signal as a sum of sinusoids:

x(t) = A·sin(2π·f·t + φ) + DC → x(t) = a₀/2 + Σₙ [ aₙ·cos(2πnf₀t) + bₙ·sin(2πnf₀t) ]

Fourier series decomposes any periodic signal into harmonics with known coefficients (square wave: bₙ = 4/(nπ), odd n; sawtooth: bₙ = −2(−1)ⁿ/(nπ); triangle: aₙ = −4/(nπ)², odd n; pulse: aₙ = (2/(nπ))·sin(nπ/4)). The DFT is the discrete, finite-length, all-purpose engine behind digital signal processing:

X[k] = Σₙ x[n]·e^{−j2πkn/N} , e^{jθ} = cos θ + j·sin θ (Euler)

where each bin k measures how much the signal “correlates” with a complex sinusoid at that frequency; magnitude = amplitude, phase = time shift. The rest of B is applications of this one transform:

  • Filtering — multiply the spectrum by a gain H(f) (low / high / band-pass) and inverse-transform: keep or kill chosen frequencies.
  • Sampling & aliasing (Nyquist–Shannon) — f_s ≥ 2·f_max; sample slower and high frequencies fold into lower ones (aliasing).
  • Wavelets — time-frequency localization: “WHAT + WHEN,” the fix Fourier needs for non-stationary signals (a scalogram shows which frequency is active when).
  • Holographic storage — an interference pattern stores amplitude and phase; the hologram is the Fourier transform of the stored data, and reconstruction by diffraction is a Fourier transform performed by light.

The bridge — one shadow, two unprojections

Read side by side, the two “big enough” theorems are the same rule in two units:

Takens: d ≥ 2m + 1 (geometric dimensions → no self-overlap / no false neighbors)
Nyquist: f_s ≥ 2·f_max (frequency bins / rate → no aliasing / no spectral leakage)

Both say: the reconstruction must be at least as rich as the object it shadows, plus a margin, or the projection cannot be undone. Below the bound, the two failure modes are the same fold: geometric folding / false neighbors (A) is the spatial face of aliasing / leakage (B). And wavelets are where the coordinate systems join: a wavelet coefficient is local-in-time (A’s geometry, the trajectory as it moves) convolved with local-in-frequency (B’s spectrum) — the time-frequency cell that a pure Fourier line (global in time) or a pure delay portrait (global in frequency) cannot see.

Fused takeaway. Give me any x(t) and I can read it three ways that are the same data: as a time-domain shadow (raw), as a space-domain attractor (delay embedding — its shape and dynamics), and as a frequency-domain spectrum (DFT — its sinusoidal ingredients). For a periodic, harmonic signal the spectrum is a few clean peaks and the embedding is a closed loop (B’s world). For a chaotic, aperiodic signal the spectrum is broadband and useless, but the embedding still unfolds the true geometry (A’s world). Choosing the right unprojection — and sizing it past its dimension bound — is the whole game.
