A Monoid Is an Adjunction Collapsed to One Object

Fusion aus: monoid.html ✖ adjunct_functor.html

Kernthese der Fusion

Both papers study the same pair of ingredients — a neutral element and a coherence law that makes a way of combining things well-defined — but they place that pair at two different levels. Paper A (monoid) places it inside a single closed set: a set of elements with a binary operation that is closed, a do-nothing identity element e, and associativity of the operation. Paper B (adjunction) places it across the boundary between two categories: a pair of functors L <-> R linked by a hom-set bijection, where the unit ηc: c → R(L(c)) and counit εd: L(R(d)) → d are the canonical “best-approximation” maps and the triangle (zig-zag) identities are the coherence law.

The single integrated position that unites them: a monoid is exactly a category with one object (its operation is composition of morphisms, its identity element e is the identity morphism, and its associativity is associativity of composition), and an adjunction is what you get when you let that one-object structure live across two interacting worlds. When you cross the boundary, the single neutral element e splits into a unit and a counit, and the single associativity law becomes the zig-zag that forces the two functors to be genuine inverses up to a canonical map.

The concrete object where the two papers literally touch is the free monoid on a set X (the word monoid X*): under concatenation it is a monoid in A’s sense (empty word ε as identity, associative), and it is the left adjoint of the forgetful functor in B’s sense, since HomMon(L(X), M) ≅ HomSet(X, U(M)). It is one object wearing two hats — and that object is the fusion.

Widersprüche zwischen A und B

There is no hard, literal contradiction: A talks about a single closed algebraic object, B about a relationship between two categories. They do not make opposing factual claims. They do, however, take incompatible stances on where structure lives and what the neutral/coherence objects do. The four sharpest tensions:

① Closure (a sealed world) vs. boundary-crossing (an open one)

A asserts closure as its first rule: “Combining two elements always produces another element in the same set.” The structure is sealed inside one set — there is nothing to leave. B has no closure at all: L : 𝕋 → 𝕊 and R : 𝕊 → 𝕋 deliberately cross the boundary between two different categories, and the adjunction is that crossing (the hom-set bijection links a 𝕊-side map L(c) → d to a 𝕋-side map c → R(d)). A’s world is a closed system; B’s world is defined by its boundary.

② A do-nothing element vs. an active approximation map

A’s identity is a passive, do-nothing member of the set: a ∘ e = e ∘ a = a. B’s closest neutral object is the identity morphism id — but id is only the seed: the unit ηc: c → R(L(c)) is “the adjunct of idL(c)” and is explicitly not do-nothing — it is “the ‘best approximation’ of c inside R’s image.” So what is passive-and-neutral in A (e) becomes active-and-structural in B (η, ε). The “do-nothing” of A is a value; the do-nothing object of B (id) generates non-neutral maps.

③ Internal regrouping vs. cross-boundary round-trip

A’s central law is associativity: regrouping one operation on three elements does not matter — a flexibility within a single operation. B’s central laws are the triangle (zig-zag) identities: a round trip through both functors collapses to the identity, εL(c) ∘ L(ηc) = idL(c) and R(εd) ∘ ηR(d) = idR(d) — a rigidity across two operations that forces L and R to be true inverses up to a canonical map. One is the looseness of a single operation; the other is the constraint that two operations genuinely cancel.

④ Symmetric & self-contained vs. asymmetric & two-sided

A presents the identity as acting identically on both sides — it even ships a “Swap (e ∘ a)” control, since a ∘ e = e ∘ a; the object is symmetric and self-contained. B is asymmetric by construction: L is the “free/left” functor and R the “forgetful/right” functor, and the bijection is directional — Hom𝕊(L(c), d) ≅ Hom𝕋(c, R(d)) — with L ⊞ R not interchangeable with R ⊞ L. There is a built-in free-vs-forgetful hierarchy that A simply does not have.

The sharpest real tension, stated as a question: Is a neutral element + a single coherence law enough, or do you need two worlds for the neutral object to become interesting? A says the neutral element is a passive ingredient of one closed set; B says the neutral object (id) only earns its keep as the generator of two active, non-neutral maps that exist only because there are two categories to move between.

Auflösung

① Sealed vs. open → one-object category vs. two categories

A is the collapsed special case of B’s world. A monoid is a category with a single object ∗, so there is exactly one object and therefore no boundary to cross — “closure” is just the statement that all hom-sets are the same set. B relaxes this by using two categories; L and R are the crossings. Reconciled: a monoid is the identity adjunction id ⊞ id on a one-object category — there the “free/forgetful” distinction vanishes because both functors are the identity.

② Passive e vs. active η/ε → the identity, transported

The identity morphism id (the seed of η/ε in B) is the monoid’s e in the one-object picture: e is exactly what id does when there is only one object. The unit and counit are “what the identity becomes under a change of world” — the best approximation of an object after you move to a different category and back. Reconciled: e = id in the collapsed world; η and ε = id transported across the boundary.

③ Associativity vs. triangles → one coherence family, two arities

Composition-associativity in a one-object category is monoid associativity. The triangle identities are the adjunction’s “round-trip = identity” laws, which reduce to trivialities in the one-object case. Both are the same kind of statement — a diagram of compositions is well-defined / commutes — differing only in how many objects are involved. Reconciled: A’s law is internal (one operation, regrouping three); B’s is external (two operations, round-trip through both).

④ Symmetric vs. asymmetric → asymmetry = the number of worlds

Collapse the adjunction to a one-object category with L = R = id and the asymmetry vanishes, recovering the symmetric monoid identity a ∘ e = e ∘ a. B’s asymmetry is exactly the information gained by having two different categories; A is the symmetric quotient of that. Reconciled: asymmetry ↔ two worlds; symmetry ↔ one world.

Fused conclusion: A monoid is the shadow an adjunction casts when its two categories are glued into one. The identity element e is the identity morphism id; associativity is associativity of composition; the do-nothing law a ∘ e = a is the collapsed triangle identity. Conversely, an adjunction is the monoid’s idea — a neutral element plus a coherent operation — lifted from one object to two interacting worlds, where the neutral element splits into unit and counit and associativity becomes the zig-zag. The free monoid is the concrete place where both are the same object.

Monoid (A) — the three rules, integrated

  • Closure: combining two elements gives another element of the same set.
  • Associativity: (a ∘ b) ∘ c = a ∘ (b ∘ c) — grouping does not matter.
  • Identity: a special e with a ∘ e = e ∘ a = a (shown symmetrically — a “swap” control).
  • Examples in A: numbers under + (e = 0), strings under concatenation (e = “”), colors under averaging-blend (e = transparent). Fused: the strings/concat example is the free monoid on the alphabet — the bridge object below.

The bridge — a monoid is a one-object category

Take a category with a single object ∗. Then Hom(∗,∗) is a set of morphisms; composing two of them is a binary operation on that set; id∗ is an identity for it; and associativity of composition is associativity of the operation. That is precisely a monoid. Reading it backwards: any monoid (M, ∘, e) is a one-object category. So A’s “set + operation + identity” and B’s “category + morphisms” are the same structure at different zoom levels.

Adjunction (B) — the definition, integrated

  • Hom-set bijection: functors L : 𝕋 → 𝕊, R : 𝕊 → 𝕋 with Hom𝕊(L(c), d) ≅ Hom𝕋(c, R(d)), natural in c and d. B labels L “free/best approximation” and R “forgetful/underlying”.
  • Unit ηc: c → R(L(c)) = adjunct of idL(c); counit εd: L(R(d)) → d = adjunct of idR(d).
  • Triangle identities: εL(c) ∘ L(ηc) = idL(c) and R(εd) ∘ ηR(d) = idR(d).
  • Examples in B: Free ⊞ Forgetful (Sets ↔ Grps), Discrete ⊞ Forgetful (Sets ↔ Top), a simple 3×3 example. Fused: “Free ⊞ Forgetful” is the exact adjunction where the free side is a free monoid.

The unified object — the free monoid L(X) = X*

Let X = {a, b}. The free monoid L(X) = X* is the set of all finite words (lists) over X. Monoid hat: the operation is concatenation, the identity is the empty word ε, and concatenation is associative — so X* is a monoid in A’s sense. Adjunction hat: there is a bijection

HomMon(L(X), M)  ≅  HomSet(X, U(M))

natural in any monoid M, where U : Mon → Set forgets the operation. A function f : X → U(M) extends uniquely to a monoid homomorphism ̄f : L(X) → M by evaluating a word with M’s operation: ̄f(x₁…x₂) = f(x₁) ∘ … ∘ f(x₂), ̄f(ε) = idM. The two unit/counit maps, concretely:

  • Unit ηX: X → U(L(X)): η(x) = [x] — wrap a bare letter in a one-letter word (“best approximation of x inside a monoid”).
  • Counit εM: L(U(M)) → M: fold a word over the underlying set of M using M’s operation (“the free word collapses into M by its own operation”).
  • Triangle, worked: on the word ab, L(η)(ab) = [a][b], then ε([a][b]) = [a] ∘ [b] = ab = id(ab) ✓.
